# Best Credit Card For General Spending

There is no single "best" card for everyone. The right pick depends on your spending habits, which travel program you like, and how many cards you already have open. But for most people building a points strategy, the best everyday card earns at least 1.5 to 2 points or cash back per dollar on purchases that do not fit a bonus category.

## Why Does My "Everyday Card" Even Matter?

Your everyday card catches every dollar that does not earn bonus points somewhere else. If that card only earns 1 point per dollar, you are leaving value on the table every single day.

Think about how you actually spend money. You have your big categories: groceries, gas, dining, travel. Good cards cover those. But then there is everything else. Insurance payments. Vet bills. Home Depot runs. Software subscriptions. Doctor copays. Tuition. That stuff can be 30% to 50% of your total spending, and it usually does not fit into any bonus category.

If you pay for all of that with a card earning 1 point per dread dollar, you are basically leaving free points on the table. Over a year, that gap adds up fast. Someone spending $2,000 a month on non-bonused purchases earns 24,000 points a year on a 1x card. On a 2x card, that is 48,000 points. Same spending, double the points, just from picking a better default card.

This is why points nerds talk so much about the "catch-all" card. It is not exciting. It does not have a flashy welcome bonus story attached to it most of the time. But it quietly does more work than almost any other card in your wallet.

## What Are My Actual Options For A Catch-All Card?

Your main options fall into three buckets: flat-rate cash back or points cards, flexible transferable-point cards, and cards tied to a specific airline or hotel. For most people chasing Chase Ultimate Rewards or similar programs, a flat 1.5x to 2x card in that same ecosystem tends to work best.

Let's break these down.

### Flat-Rate Cards

These cards earn the same rate on every purchase, no matter the category. Examples of this type include cards earning 1.5x, 2x, or even 2.5x points or cash back on everything.

The appeal here is simple. You never have to think about categories. You never have to check if this month's bonus category applies. You swipe, you earn, you move on.

The tradeoff is that flat-rate cards usually earn less than a card's own bonus categories would pay in a good month. A 3x dining card beats a 2x flat card at restaurants. But most of your spending is not in a bonus category, so the flat card wins on average.

### Cards Tied To A Points Program

If you already collect Chase Ultimate Rewards, Amex Membership Rewards, or Capital One miles, it often makes sense to keep your catch-all card in that same family. This keeps all your points in one pot, which makes them easier to use for a big redemption later.

For example, someone deep in the Chase ecosystem might use a card like the Chase Sapphire Preferred or Ink Business Preferred as their catch-all, since both earn Ultimate Rewards points that combine with points from other Chase cards. Someone in the Amex world might lean on a card like Amex Blue Business Plus, which earns 2x Membership Rewards points on the first $50,000 spent per year, then 1x after that.

Mixing programs is not wrong. But it does mean you end up with smaller piles of points in different accounts instead of one big pile you can use for a serious trip.

### Cash Back Cards

Not everyone wants to deal with transfer partners and award charts. If that is you, a flat cash back card can be a perfectly good catch-all. Cards earning 2% cash back on everything are common, and 2% is a real, easy-to-understand return.

The math is simple: $30,000 a year in spending on a 2% card is $600 back, no research required. Compare that to trying to squeeze extra value out of transferable points, which takes time and some luck with transfer partner availability.

## How Does This Fit Into A Chase 5/24 Strategy?

Your catch-all card choice should support your card application plan, not fight against it. This matters most for people managing their 5/24 count, since the order you apply for cards can affect which ones you can still get later.

Here is the thing many people get wrong. They get excited about a big welcome bonus card, use up an application slot, and then grab whatever cash back card is advertised in their bank's app for daily spending. That works, but it is not always the most efficient path.

A more common approach among people watching their 5/24 count closely is to prioritize Chase cards while under 5/24, since Chase cuts off most personal card approvals once you have opened 5 or more cards (from any bank) in the past 24 months. Business cards from Chase also count toward your 5/24 total in most cases, even though they are business cards.

This means your catch-all card decision can double as a 5/24 decision. If you are still under 5/24 and want to build a stack of Chase cards, picking a Chase card as your everyday spender keeps your points in the Ultimate Rewards ecosystem and locks in a valuable seat before you age out of eligibility.

If you are already over 5/24, this changes things. You are locked out of most new Chase personal cards regardless of what else you do. In that case, your everyday card choice opens back up. American Express, Capital One, and Citi do not use the same 5/24 rule, so you have more freedom to pick a catch-all card from those issuers without worrying about Chase eligibility.

## Should I Use One Card Or Split Spending Across Two?

Many people find it useful to run two cards for everyday spending: one flat-rate card for non-bonused purchases, and one bonus-category card for their biggest recurring expense. This setup covers more ground than a single card can.

Say your biggest monthly expense is groceries. A card earning 3x or more at supermarkets on a specific card, paired with a 2x flat card for everything else, usually beats a single 2x card across the board. You are matching each dollar to whichever card pays the most for that category.

The tradeoff is complexity. Two cards means two things to keep track of, two annual fees to consider, and more decisions at checkout. If you already track spending closely or use an app to remind you which card to pull out, this is not a big deal. If you would rather not think about it, a single strong flat-rate card removes that friction entirely.

There is no wrong answer here. It depends on how much mental energy you want to spend optimizing versus how much value you are willing to give up for simplicity.

## What About Business Cards For Everyday Spending?

If you have any self-employment income, freelance work, or a side business, a business card can be a strong catch-all option, since some business cards earn higher flat rates than their personal counterparts. The Amex Blue Business Plus example from earlier is a good case of this.

You do not need an LLC or a formal business to qualify for most business cards. Sole proprietors, freelancers, and even people who sell stuff on eBay regularly can often qualify using their own name as the business name and their Social Security number for the application.

Business cards carry a side benefit for 5/24 purposes too. Cards from American Express, Capital One, and Citi generally do not report to your personal credit report, and in most cases they do not count against your Chase 5/24 total either, depending on the issuer. Chase business cards are the exception here, since they do count toward 5/24 even though they are business products. This makes non-Chase business cards a useful tool for adding a strong catch-all card without touching your 5/24 count at all.

## So Which Card Should I Actually Pick?

Start by figuring out where your money already goes. Look at last month's statement and find your biggest chunk of spending that does not fit a bonus category. That answer points you toward the right catch-all card faster than any list of recommendations.

If your non-bonused spending is heavy and you want simplicity, a strong flat-rate cash back card removes decision fatigue completely. If you are actively building a points balance for a specific goal like a flight or hotel stay, a flat-rate card inside your main points program keeps things consolidated and easier to use later.

If you are still under 5/24, lean toward Chase products while that door is open, since it may close for good once you cross the line. If you are already over 5/24, look at Amex, Capital One, or Citi flat-rate cards instead, since those doors stay open regardless of your Chase status.

Whatever you choose, the goal is the same. Stop letting your biggest chunk of spending earn the lowest rate in your wallet. That single fix does more for your points balance than chasing another bonus category ever will.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best credit card for everyday general spending?
The best general spending card depends on your goals, but top picks include the Chase Sapphire Preferred, Citi Double Cash, and Capital One Venture X for flexible rewards. Cash-back seekers often prefer flat 2% cards like the Double Cash, while points-focused travelers benefit more from transferable currencies like Chase Ultimate Rewards. Consider your credit profile, spending categories, and whether you're actively working around the Chase 5/24 rule before applying, since sequencing matters for maximizing sign-up bonuses.

### Does applying for a general spending card count toward Chase 5/24?
Yes, any new personal credit card, regardless of issuer, counts toward your Chase 5/24 total. Chase tracks how many personal cards you've opened across all banks in the past 24 months, not just Chase cards. So if you're planning to apply for a Chase card later, it's smart to prioritize Chase products first before opening cards from Amex, Capital One, or other issuers to avoid accidentally going over the 5/24 threshold.

### Should I prioritize Chase cards before other issuers for general spending?
Yes, if a Chase card is on your target list, apply for it before other bank cards. Because Chase 5/24 blocks approval once you've opened five or more cards in 24 months, sequencing applications strategically protects your eligibility. Get your Chase Sapphire Preferred or Ink Business card approved first, then branch out to Amex, Citi, or Capital One general spending cards, which don't have similar restrictive rules based on overall application count.

### How do sign-up bonuses affect the value of a general spending card?
Sign-up bonuses often provide more value than a year of regular spending rewards, making them critical to card selection. A card offering 60,000-80,000 points after meeting a minimum spend requirement can be worth $600-$1,200+ depending on redemption method. When choosing a general spending card, factor in the bonus size, required spending threshold, and annual fee alongside the ongoing rewards rate to determine true long-term value.

### Is a flat-rate cash-back card better than a points card for general spending?
It depends on whether you value simplicity or maximized redemption value. Flat-rate cash-back cards like the Citi Double Cash offer straightforward 2% returns with no category tracking. Points cards, especially those earning transferable currency like Chase Ultimate Rewards or Amex Membership Rewards, can yield higher value through travel transfers but require more effort. Choose cash-back for simplicity or points if you're willing to optimize redemptions for outsized travel value.

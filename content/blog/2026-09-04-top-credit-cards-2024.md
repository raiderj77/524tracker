# Top Credit Cards for 2024 (And How They Fit Your 5/24 Strategy)

**Quick answer:** The best 2024 cards for points nerds are Chase Sapphire Preferred, Chase Ink Business Unlimited/Cash, Amex Gold, Amex Platinum, and Capital One Venture X. If you're still under 5/24, prioritize Chase cards first. If you're over 5/24, focus on Amex and Capital One products instead.

## Why does 5/24 change the order I should apply in?

If you're under 5/24, Chase cards should almost always come first. Once you cross 5/24, Chase is off the table until you drop back under it, so those cards need to happen early.

This is the single biggest mistake new hobbyists make. They grab an Amex or Capital One card first, not knowing that two or three cards from other banks can push them over 5/24. Then they're locked out of Chase for years.

Here's the rule again, quickly, since it drives every decision below: Chase counts personal cards opened at any bank in the last 24 months. If you have 5 or more, Chase will likely deny you for a new card. Business cards from most banks don't count, but Chase's own business cards still show up on some reports depending on the bank that issues them and how they report.

So the smart move in 2024 is the same as it's been for years: sequence your applications. Chase first, then everything else.

## What are the best Chase cards to get before you hit 5/24?

The Chase Sapphire Preferred and the Chase Ink Business cards are the two most valuable pulls for most people. They offer strong bonuses, low annual fees relative to their value, and points that transfer to a wide range of airlines and hotels.

**Chase Sapphire Preferred**
This card has been a staple for over a decade. It earns Chase Ultimate Rewards points, which transfer to airline partners like United, Southwest, and Air France/KLM, plus hotel partners like Hyatt and Marriott. The annual fee sits around $95. Many people find the sign-up bonus alone can be worth several hundred dollars in travel, depending on how the points get redeemed.

**Chase Sapphire Reserve**
The bigger sibling to the Preferred. Higher annual fee (around $550), but it comes with a $300 annual travel credit that offsets a chunk of that fee. Points earn at a better rate on travel and dining, and you get airport lounge access through Priority Pass. This card makes more sense once you're already comfortable with the ecosystem and travel enough to use the credits.

**Chase Ink Business Unlimited and Ink Business Cash**
These are no-annual-fee business cards. "Business" doesn't mean you need an LLC. A sole proprietorship works, think freelancing, selling stuff online, or driving for a rideshare app. These cards typically don't count against your personal 5/24 count in the same way, and Chase business cards generally don't report to your personal credit report at all, which matters for your utilization and other card issuers looking at your file.

The Ink Cash earns 5% on the first $25,000 spent at office supply stores and on phone, internet, and cable services each year. The Ink Unlimited earns a flat 1.5% on everything. Both have earned strong sign-up bonuses historically.

**Chase Ink Business Preferred**
This one has an annual fee (around $95) but earns 3x points on the first $150,000 spent across categories like travel, shipping, internet, cable, phone services, and advertising with social media sites and search engines. The points here are the same valuable Ultimate Rewards currency as the Sapphire cards.

## What should I get once I'm over 5/24?

Consider the American Express Gold Card for its generous dining and supermarket rewards. It offers 4x points at restaurants and up to $25,000 in annual grocery spending at 4x before switching to 1x. The annual fee is $250.


Once you're past 5/24, Amex and Capital One become your main playground. Both have their own rules, but neither cares about your Chase-reported card count.

**American Express Gold Card**
This card earns 4x points at restaurants and at US supermarkets (up to $25,000 per year in the supermarket category, then 1x after that). The annual fee is $250. Amex Membership Rewards points transfer to airlines like Delta, Air France/KLM, and British Airways, plus hotel programs like Marriott and Hilton.

Amex has its own version of 5/24 called the "once per lifetime" bonus rule. If you've already gotten a welcome bonus on a specific card, you generally can't get that same bonus again. Amex also uses a soft rule sometimes referred to as 2/90, limiting how many new Amex cards you can open in a rolling 90-day window, and there are rumors of a 1/5 rule (one new card per 5 days) too, though Amex has never confirmed these publicly.

**American Express Platinum Card**
The prestige card of the points world. Annual fee is $695. That's steep, but it comes loaded with credits: airline incidental credits, Uber Cash, hotel status, Global Entry or TSA PreCheck fee credit, and access to Centurion Lounges and Priority Pass lounges. Many people find they only get value if they actually use the credits, so this card rewards people who travel often and are organized about tracking their perks.

**American Express Business Platinum**
Similar structure to the personal Platinum but aimed at small business owners. It has a higher earning rate on airfare and on purchases over $5,000 (a lot of ordinary businesses hit that fast if they're buying inventory or equipment). Like other Amex business cards, this one requires some kind of business activity, even informal.

**Capital One Venture X**
This card has become one of the most talked-about cards since it launched in 2021. Annual fee is $395, but it comes with a $300 annual travel credit booked through Capital One's travel portal, plus 10,000 bonus miles every year on your account anniversary. That anniversary bonus alone is worth roughly $100 in travel value, which nearly cancels out the rest of the fee.

Capital One miles transfer to a solid list of airline partners, including Air Canada Aeroplan, Avianca LifeMiles, and Turkish Airlines Miles&Smiles, three programs that points nerds like because of their sweet spot awards on partner airlines.

Capital One also has its own version of a velocity rule. It's less well-documented than Chase's 5/24, but the general pattern people report is that Capital One looks at how many new accounts you've opened recently across all banks, not just their own. This makes application order matter here too, though the exact thresholds aren't public.

## Are annual fee cards actually worth it in 2024?

For many people, yes, if they use the credits and travel benefits that come with the card. If you're not going to use the credits, a no-fee card usually makes more sense.

The math here isn't complicated once you break it down. Take the Amex Platinum's $695 fee. If you fly Delta or another partner airline often enough to use the $200 airline incidental credit, use Uber Cash every month, and get value from lounge access, the card can pay for itself many times over.

But if you don't travel much, or you're not going to remember to redeem a $100 hotel credit before it expires, that same card becomes a bad deal fast. This is why so many experienced points collectors keep a spreadsheet of their card fees and renewal dates. It sounds excessive, but it's the only reliable way to know if a card is still earning its keep.

A simple rule many people use: add up the credits you'll actually use, not the credits that exist on paper. If that number covers most or all of the annual fee, the card is probably worth keeping.

## How many cards should I actually apply for in 2024?

There's no single right number. It depends on how close you are to 5/24, how your credit score is doing, and how much new debt or inquiries you're comfortable adding to your file.

A common approach in the hobby is to space out applications by a few months rather than applying for several cards in the same week. This gives your credit score time to recover between hard pulls and lets you meet each card's minimum spending requirement without stretching your budget too thin.

Minimum spend requirements vary a lot. Some cards ask for $3,000 in the first three months. Others ask for $6,000 or more. Stacking too many of these at once can turn a fun hobby into a stressful spending scramble, so pace yourself based on your normal monthly spending, not the other way around.

It's also worth checking your full credit report before applying for anything. Errors happen more often than people expect, and a mistake on your report can tank an approval you'd otherwise get easily.

## What's the biggest mistake to avoid this year?

The biggest mistake is applying for cards in the wrong order. Getting a Capital One or Amex card before locking in your Chase cards is the classic error that costs people years of missed bonuses.

Beyond that, chasing every new card announcement without a plan is a close second. New cards get hyped constantly on forums and social media. Not every hyped card fits every person's spending pattern or travel goals. A card that's perfect for someone who flies Delta twenty times a year might be useless for someone who mostly drives for work and rarely flies.

Before applying for anything in 2024, it helps to ask three questions. Where am I on 5/24 right now? What am I actually trying to earn, cash back, a specific airline's miles, or flexible points? And will I really use the perks this card offers, or am I just applying because everyone online is talking about it?

Answering those honestly will do more for your points strategy than any single card on this list.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is Chase's 5/24 rule?
Chase's 5/24 rule means you'll likely be denied for a new Chase credit card if you've opened 5 or more personal credit cards (from any bank) in the past 24 months. This unofficial policy applies to most Chase consumer and business cards, including popular options like the Sapphire Preferred and Ink Business Preferred. Chase counts nearly all personal cards toward this limit, even store cards and authorized user accounts in some cases. Because of this, savvy applicants prioritize Chase cards before pursuing multiple cards from other issuers to stay under the threshold.

### Which cards should I apply for first if I'm under 5/24?
Prioritize Chase cards first, since they're the most restrictive about your application history. If you're eligible for both a personal and business Chase card, apply for those before branching out to Amex, Citi, or Capital One, which generally have more lenient approval rules. A common strategy is applying for the Chase Sapphire Preferred or Ink Business Unlimited early, then filling in with other issuers' bonuses afterward. This sequencing maximizes your chances of getting Chase's best sign-up bonuses before your application count rises.

### How many credit cards can I apply for at once?
There's no universal limit, but applying for more than 2 cards within a few months can trigger fraud alerts or hurt approval odds. Issuers like Chase, Amex, and Capital One each have their own velocity rules limiting how many cards they'll approve in a short window. For example, Amex generally limits applicants to 2 approvals within a rolling 90-day period. Spacing applications 3-6 months apart, while tracking your 5/24 status, generally yields better approval rates and protects your credit score from excessive inquiries.

### Do sign-up bonuses really pay off compared to the annual fee?
Yes, in most cases, a card's welcome bonus alone can exceed the annual fee's value many times over. For example, a 60,000-point bonus worth $600-900 in travel value easily outweighs a $95 annual fee, even after just one year of use. The key is meeting the minimum spending requirement naturally through everyday purchases rather than overspending. After the first year, evaluate ongoing perks like lounge access, travel credits, or point-earning categories to decide whether keeping the card long-term still makes financial sense.

### How long should I wait between credit card applications?
Most experienced points collectors wait 3-6 months between new credit card applications to protect their credit score and approval odds. This spacing helps average out hard inquiries, allows utilization ratios to normalize, and gives issuers time to see healthy account management. Some card issuers, like Chase and Amex, also have specific bonus eligibility rules requiring a set time since your last bonus on the same card. Tracking your application dates on a tool like 524tracker.com helps ensure you're not applying too aggressively.

# Best Credit Cards for April 2024: A Step-by-Step Guide to Picking Your Next Card

Right now, strong picks include the Chase Sapphire Preferred for travel, the Amex Blue Business Plus for no-fee business spending, and the Citi Double Cash for flat-rate cash back. But "best" depends on your 5/24 status, your goals, and what bonus categories you already have covered. Follow the steps below to find your best fit.

## Step 1: What's My Chase 5/24 Status Right Now?

Count how many personal credit cards you've opened in the last 24 months, from any bank. If that number is under 5, Chase cards are likely on the table. If it's 5 or more, you need a different plan for April.

This step comes first because it decides everything else. Chase approvals depend heavily on this rule. Business cards from most banks don't count against you here, but authorized user cards often do count, so check your full list.

Pull your credit reports if you're not sure. Many people find it easiest to keep a simple spreadsheet with card names and open dates. Sort that list by date. Count backward 24 months from today. Anything inside that window counts toward your 5/24 total.

If you're at 3/24 or 4/24, this month might be your last easy shot at a Chase card for a while. If you're at 5/24 or above, skip ahead to Step 3, where non-Chase options take center stage.

## Step 2: Which Chase Card Fits Me If I'm Under 5/24?

If travel is your goal, the Chase Sapphire Preferred is a strong choice this month. If you run a small business or side hustle, the Ink Business Preferred often carries a bigger bonus. Pick based on how you'll actually use the points, not just the sign-up offer size.

The Sapphire Preferred earns points that transfer to airline and hotel partners like United, Southwest, Hyatt, and IHG. It also earns bonus points on travel and dining. That flexibility makes it a common first pick for people building a travel points strategy.

The Ink Business Preferred is built for spending on things like internet, phone, shipping, and advertising. It earns 3 points per dollar on the first $150,000 spent yearly in those categories combined. Since it's a business card, it usually doesn't count against your personal 5/24 total, though it can still show up on your credit report.

If you already have a Sapphire card, Chase's rules limit you to one Sapphire-branded card at a time, and there are rules about how often you can earn a new bonus on the same card. Check your card's terms before applying for a repeat.

A common approach is to apply for your most valuable Chase card first, since each approval uses up part of your 5/24 room. Save weaker offers for later, once you're past the point where 5/24 matters as much.

## Step 3: What If I'm Already at 5/24 or Above?

If Chase is off the table, shift your attention to Amex, Citi, and Capital One. These issuers don't use the same 5/24 rule, so approvals depend more on your income, credit score, and existing relationship with them.

American Express looks closely at how many welcome bonuses you've already earned on a card. Their rule of thumb, often called "once per lifetime," means you generally can't get a new bonus on a card you've had before. This differs from Chase's rule, which looks at how many cards you've opened, not which specific bonuses you've claimed.

Capital One has its own limit, often described as one approval every six months across their consumer card lineup. If you got a Capital One Venture card in January, applying again in April likely won't work, since that's less than six months later.

Citi also has application rules, including limits on how many Citi cards you can open in a rolling period and rules about earning repeat bonuses on the same card within 48 months. Their rules have changed over time, so check Citi's current terms before you apply.

Business cards remain a solid path forward here too. Amex Business Gold, Amex Business Platinum, and Capital One Spark cards don't touch your Chase 5/24 count, and many of them offer strong bonuses independent of your personal card history.

## Step 4: How Do I Compare Sign-Up Bonuses Fairly?

Don't just look at the point total. Divide the bonus by the spending requirement to see the real value per dollar spent, then compare that against the annual fee.

A card offering 80,000 points after $4,000 in spending sounds bigger than one offering 60,000 points after $3,000. But run the math. The first card gives you 20 points per dollar spent to hit the bonus. The second gives you the same 20 points per dollar. They're equal in this measure, even though the headline numbers look different.

Next, think about what those points are worth to you. Many hobbyists estimate transferable points, like Chase Ultimate Rewards or Amex Membership Rewards, in the range of 1 to 2 cents each when used for travel transfers. Cash back cards are simpler, since $200 is always $200.

Also factor in the annual fee. A card with a $95 fee and a 60,000-point bonus might beat a $550 annual fee card with an 80,000-point bonus, especially if you won't use the expensive card's travel credits and lounge access. Many people find that fee-light cards make more sense outside their first year or two in the hobby.

Finally, check the minimum spend window. Most bonuses require spending a set amount within 3 months of account opening. Make sure that spending requirement fits naturally into your budget. Stretching your budget just to hit a bonus is a common mistake that leads to overspending.

## Step 5: How Do I Time My Applications This Month?

Apply for your most valuable, hardest-to-get card first, then work down to easier approvals. This order protects your credit inquiries and your 5/24 slots for the cards that matter most.

If you're planning to apply for both a Chase card and an Amex card this month, apply for the Chase card first. Amex approvals don't get harder as your 5/24 count rises, but Chase approvals do. There's no reason to burn a Chase slot on a lower-value card if a better one is still available to you.

Space out your applications by at least a few days to a couple weeks when possible. Some banks review your recent application activity across all issuers, not just their own. A sudden burst of five new accounts in one week looks different to an underwriter than the same five accounts spread across two months.

Check for pre-qualification tools before applying. Chase, Amex, Citi, and Capital One all offer online pre-qualification checks that use a soft pull, meaning it won't affect your credit score. These aren't guarantees of approval, but they can rule out obviously bad timing.

Also watch for elevated public offers. Card bonuses change often, and April sometimes brings limited-time increases on specific cards, especially around tax season when issuers expect higher spending. Check the official card page directly rather than relying on outdated numbers from old blog posts or forum threads.

## Step 6: How Do I Meet Minimum Spend Without Overspending?

Time your application around planned purchases you already know are coming, like taxes, insurance premiums, or a big planned trip. This lets natural spending cover most or all of the requirement without forcing extra purchases.

Look at your calendar for the next three months. Big known expenses, like car insurance paid twice a year, property tax bills, or annual subscriptions, are common ways people naturally reach a spending requirement.

Many people also fund everyday spending through their new card temporarily, things like groceries, gas, and utility bills. Since money still leaves your account either way, shifting the payment method to a new card costs nothing extra as long as you pay the statement in full.

Some payment services also let you pay certain bills, like rent or taxes, with a credit card for a small processing fee, often around 1.5% to 2.9%. Whether that fee is worth it depends on the value of the points you'll earn compared to the fee cost. Run the math before committing to this method.

Avoid buying things you don't need just to hit a bonus threshold. This is one of the most common regrets in the points hobby. A $500 bonus item you didn't need can easily erase the value of the sign-up bonus you were chasing.

## Step 7: How Do I Track Everything So I Don't Lose Track of 5/24?

Keep a running log of every card you open, the date, the bank, and whether it's personal or business. Update it the same day you get approved, so you're never guessing your current 5/24 count.

A simple spreadsheet works fine. Columns for card name, issuer, open date, annual fee, and bonus met are usually enough. Some hobbyists also use free tools like Award Wallet to track points balances across multiple loyalty programs in one place.

Set a calendar reminder for each card's annual fee date. This helps you decide whether to keep the card, downgrade it to a no-fee version, or cancel it before the fee hits again. Many people find that reviewing their full card lineup once a year, around the same month each time, keeps their strategy organized.

Also track your minimum spend deadlines separately from your 5/24 count. Missing a bonus deadline by a few days can mean losing the entire bonus, even if you spent the required amount just after the cutoff.

## Step 8: What Should I Do Next After This Month's Application?

Wait for your statement to close, confirm your bonus posted, and then start planning your next application 3 to 6 months out. This spacing keeps your credit profile healthy and gives you time to plan your next spending category around a new card.

Check your account online after your minimum spend period ends. Bonuses typically post within 1 to 2 billing cycles after you meet the requirement. If it hasn't shown up after that window, contact the bank directly through secure message or phone.

Use this waiting period to research your next target card. Look at upcoming personal expenses, travel plans, or business purchases that might line up well with a future bonus. Many people keep a running wish list of cards they want next, ranked by value and by how urgently they need to apply before hitting 5/24.

Review your full card lineup every few months. Drop or downgrade cards that no longer earn their annual fee back in value. This keeps your credit file clean and your total card count manageable, which matters both for approval odds and for your own sanity when tracking bonuses.

## Frequently asked questions

### What credit card should I apply for first if I'm under 5/24?
Start with a Chase card if you plan to eventually get multiple Chase products, since approval depends on having fewer than 5 new accounts in 24 months. Prioritizing Chase early, like the Sapphire Preferred or Ink Business Preferred, lets you lock in strong sign-up bonuses before other issuers' cards push you over the limit. Once you're past 5/24, you can still apply for Amex, Citi, and Capital One cards, but Chase approvals become far harder to secure.

### What is the best credit card for a large sign-up bonus in April 2024?
The Chase Sapphire Preferred and Chase Ink Business Preferred currently offer some of the strongest bonuses, often 60,000-100,000 points after meeting a minimum spend requirement. These bonuses can be worth $750-$1,500+ in travel value depending on redemption method. Business cards are especially valuable since they don't count against personal credit utilization and often have easier-to-meet spending thresholds relative to their bonus size.

### How does the Chase 5/24 rule affect my application strategy?
The 5/24 rule means Chase will likely deny you for any new card if you've opened 5 or more personal credit cards (from any issuer) in the past 24 months. This makes application order critical, apply for Chase cards before other issuers to avoid getting locked out. Authorized user accounts and some business cards may or may not count depending on the bank, so checking your credit report count before applying is essential.

### Should I get a personal or business credit card for points?
Business cards are often the smarter choice if you have any self-employment, side hustle, or freelance income, since they typically offer bonuses comparable to personal cards without counting toward Chase's 5/24 limit as heavily. Many business cards also report only to your personal credit in limited ways, keeping utilization lower. If you don't have qualifying business activity, personal cards remain the more accessible option for building a points strategy.

### How many credit cards should I apply for at once in April 2024?
Most experts recommend applying for no more than 1-2 cards per app session, spaced a few months apart, to protect your credit score and approval odds. Applying for too many cards simultaneously can trigger issuer scrutiny, lower your average account age, and cause multiple hard inquiries that temporarily dent your score. A measured approach, prioritizing Chase cards first if you're near the 5/24 threshold, maximizes long-term bonus potential.

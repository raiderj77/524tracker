# Best Credit Card for Multiple Categories: A Step-by-Step Guide

Building a wallet that covers multiple bonus categories takes planning, not luck. The short answer: pair a flat-rate card for everything else with two or three category cards that match your biggest spending buckets, like groceries, gas, and dining. Then rotate or stack them so nothing earns at a low rate.

## Step 1: What Are Your Biggest Spending Categories?

Look at three months of statements before you pick any card. You cannot build the right stack until you know where your money actually goes.

Most people guess wrong about their own spending. You might think you spend more on travel than groceries, but your bank statement often tells a different story. Pull up your last three months of transactions. Sort them into buckets: groceries, gas, dining, travel, streaming, online shopping, and everything else.

Write down a rough monthly total for each bucket. Even a rough number helps. If groceries are $600 a month and gas is $150, that tells you which categories deserve a dedicated card. A card that earns 4x on gas does you little good if you spend $40 a month on gas and $700 on groceries.

This step feels basic, but skipping it is the number one reason people end up with cards that do not match their life.

## Step 2: Should You Start With a Flat-Rate Card or a Category Card?

Start with one flat-rate card as your base, then add category cards on top. The flat-rate card acts as a safety net for anything that does not fit a bonus category.

A flat-rate card, like one that earns 2x on every purchase, is the glue that holds a category strategy together. Category cards are great for groceries or gas, but they often drop to 1x on things like insurance payments, tuition, or a plumber. Without a flat-rate card in your wallet, that spending earns the lowest possible rate.

Many people find it easier to build the base first. Once you have a card that earns a steady 1.5x to 2x on everything, you can layer category cards on top without worrying about gaps. Think of the flat-rate card as your floor. Category cards raise you above that floor only in specific spots.

## Step 3: How Do You Match Cards to Categories Without Overlap?

Pick one card per category, not two or three that compete with each other. Overlap wastes card slots and makes tracking harder.

This is where a lot of people get tripped up. It feels smart to carry three different cards that all earn well on dining, just in case. In practice, this makes your wallet confusing. You end up asking "wait, which card gives me the bonus here?" at the register.

A cleaner approach is to assign one clear winner per category:

- Groceries: a card with a strong grocery multiplier, often 3x to 6x depending on the issuer
- Gas: a card with a gas-specific bonus, often 2x to 5x
- Dining: a card that earns extra at restaurants, including takeout and delivery in many cases
- Everything else: your flat-rate base card

Some issuers offer bonus categories that rotate quarterly, like 5x on categories that change every three months. These can fit into your stack too, but they need more attention since you have to activate them and track what quarter it is.

Once you assign a clear winner per category, using the right card becomes a habit instead of a math problem every time you check out.

## Step 4: How Many Cards Do You Actually Need?

Most people can cover their top categories with three to five cards total. More than that usually adds complexity without adding real value.

It is tempting to collect a card for every category that exists. Some cards reward office supply stores. Others reward streaming services. Others reward home improvement stores. If you chase every category, you end up with eight or nine cards and forget which one to use where.

A more common approach:

1. One flat-rate card for general spending
2. One grocery card
3. One gas card
4. One dining card
5. Maybe one rotating-category card if you like tracking activations

That is five cards. For most households, that already covers 80% to 90% of monthly spending at a bonus rate. Adding a sixth or seventh card for a category you spend $30 a month on rarely moves the needle enough to justify the mental overhead.

If you are new to this, three cards is a perfectly reasonable starting point. You can always add more once the first three feel automatic.

## Step 5: How Does Chase 5/24 Affect Your Category Card Stack?

If you are pursuing Chase cards as part of your stack, sequence your applications so Chase cards come first. Chase counts most personal credit cards opened by any bank in the last 24 months, so cards from other issuers can quietly use up your five slots.

This matters a lot for anyone building a multi-category wallet. Say you want two Chase cards for your grocery and dining categories, plus an Amex card for gas. If you open the Amex card first, and a couple other cards from different banks over the next year or two, you might cross five new accounts before you ever apply for the Chase cards you actually wanted.

A common approach is to map out your full target list before opening anything. Decide which cards you want from Chase specifically. Apply for those first, while your 24-month count is low. Then move on to cards from Amex, Citi, Capital One, or other issuers that do not use the same rule.

This is not about approval guarantees. Chase reviews applications case by case, and being under 5/24 does not guarantee approval. But applying while you are under the count removes one obstacle before other factors come into play, like income and credit history.

## Step 6: How Do You Track Which Card Earns Where?

Use a simple written list or note on your phone, not memory. Categories and multipliers change often enough that memory alone leads to mistakes.

Once you have three, four, or five cards each tied to a category, you need a system to remember which is which. A lot of people use a basic note on their phone with a short list, something like:

- Groceries: Card A
- Gas: Card B
- Dining: Card C
- Everything else: Card D

Some people go further and label their physical cards with a small sticker or tag. Others use apps built for this exact purpose that show your best card at checkout based on the merchant category. Either method works. The point is to remove the guesswork.

This step matters more once you add a rotating-category card into the mix. Those cards change their bonus category every quarter, and it is easy to keep swiping the old category out of habit. Set a calendar reminder near the start of each quarter to check the new categories and activate them if the card requires it.

## Step 7: How Do You Handle Categories That Don't Fit Neatly?

Default back to your flat-rate card for anything that does not clearly match a category. Do not force a purchase into the wrong bucket just to chase a bonus.

Real life spending rarely sorts itself into clean boxes. Is a grocery delivery app a grocery purchase or a dining purchase? Is a big box store that sells both groceries and general merchandise coded as a grocery store or a general merchandise store? Card issuers use merchant category codes set by the merchant, not by you, so the same store might code differently depending on location or how they set up their payment system.

Rather than guessing, many people find it easier to default to their flat-rate card whenever they are unsure. It is a small trade-off. You might miss out on a bonus rate here and there, but you avoid the frustration of expecting a bonus and not getting one.

If a purchase repeatedly earns at a bonus rate you did not expect, that is useful information. Note it down. Over time you will build a personal sense of which stores code which way, based on your own statements.

## Step 8: When Should You Reassess Your Card Stack?

Review your stack once a year or after a major life change, like a move, a new job, or a big shift in spending habits. Categories that made sense two years ago might not fit anymore.

Card issuers change their bonus categories and terms over time. A card that once earned well on gas might shift its focus. Your own spending changes too. Maybe you used to eat out three times a week and now you cook at home more. Maybe you moved somewhere and now drive twice as far to work, so gas spending jumped.

A simple annual check works well for most people. Pull your statements again, like you did in Step 1. Compare your current spending pattern to the categories your cards actually reward. If there is a mismatch, it might be time to swap a card out.

This is also a good moment to think about annual fees. A category card only pays for itself if the rewards you earn are worth more than the fee you pay. If your spending in that category dropped, the math might not work anymore.

## Step 9: How Do You Avoid Overcomplicating Your Wallet?

Keep your system simple enough that you can explain it in one sentence. If you cannot describe your card stack quickly, it is probably too complicated.

The biggest risk with a multi-category strategy is turning something useful into a chore. If you need a spreadsheet with twelve tabs just to remember which card to use at the pharmacy, the system has gotten away from you.

A good test: can you say your setup out loud in one breath? Something like, "I use my grocery card at the store, my gas card at the pump, my dining card at restaurants, and my flat card for everything else." If your real setup sounds more complicated than that, it is worth simplifying.

Fewer cards, used consistently, almost always beat a large pile of cards where a few sit forgotten in a drawer. A category card only helps if you actually use it in the right spot every time.

## Putting the Steps Together

Building a wallet for multiple categories is not about grabbing every good card you see. It is about matching a small number of cards to your real spending, sequencing your applications wisely if Chase cards are part of the mix, and keeping the system simple enough to actually use. Start with your spending data, build outward from a solid flat-rate base, and revisit the plan once a year to keep it matched to your life.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best credit card for multiple bonus categories?
The Chase Sapphire Preferred and American Express Gold Card are top picks for multiple bonus categories in 2024. The Sapphire Preferred earns 3x on dining and select streaming, 2x on travel, plus a strong sign-up bonus. The Amex Gold earns 4x on dining and U.S. supermarkets. Both cards count toward your Chase 5/24 status differently, so if you're pursuing multiple Chase cards, apply for Chase products before Amex to avoid 5/24 restrictions blocking future approvals.

### How does the Chase 5/24 rule affect applying for multiple credit cards?
The 5/24 rule blocks approval for most Chase cards if you've opened 5+ personal credit cards (any bank) in the past 24 months. This makes sequencing critical when building a multi-category card strategy. Since non-Chase cards still count against your 5/24 total, experienced applicants typically apply for Chase cards first, then move to Amex, Citi, or Capital One products that don't have this restriction, maximizing approval odds across issuers.

### Should I get multiple credit cards to cover different spending categories?
Yes, pairing 2-3 complementary cards typically maximizes rewards better than relying on one card. For example, combining a card with rotating 5% categories, one with fixed dining/grocery bonuses, and a flat-rate card for everything else covers most spending efficiently. This "category stacking" approach requires tracking which card to use where, but it significantly increases points and cash-back earnings compared to using a single general rewards card for all purchases.

### What's the best order to apply for credit cards to maximize sign-up bonuses?
Apply for Chase cards first, since the 5/24 rule can permanently block you once you hit five new accounts in 24 months. After securing desired Chase cards, move to Amex, Citi, Capital One, and other issuers with more flexible or velocity-based rules. Space applications 2-3 months apart to protect your credit score and approval odds, and prioritize cards with the highest-value sign-up bonuses relative to their minimum spending requirements.

### Can I still get approved for credit cards if I'm already over 5/24?
Yes, but your options narrow to issuers without hard 5/24-style restrictions, like Amex, Citi, Capital One, and Discover. These issuers have their own rules (such as Amex's once-per-lifetime bonus restriction or Citi's 48-month rule), but they don't universally block approval based on recent account openings like Chase does. Being over 5/24 means prioritizing non-Chase cards for new sign-up bonuses until your count drops back below five within a rolling 24-month window.

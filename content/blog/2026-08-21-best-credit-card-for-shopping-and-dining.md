# Best Credit Card For Shopping And Dining: Common Mistakes That Cost You Points

There is no single best card for shopping and dining. The right pick depends on which bank's ecosystem you're building and whether you value flexible points or simple cash back. Most 5/24-aware players end up running two or three cards together, not just one.

## Am I Picking One Card When I Should Be Picking a System?

Yes, if you are only carrying one card for shopping and dining, you are leaving points on the table. A single card almost never covers every category well. A better approach is pairing two or three cards that cover different bonus categories.

This mistake happens because people want things simple. One card feels easier to track. But card issuers design their bonus categories to be narrow on purpose. The Chase Freedom Flex might give 3% on dining but only 1% on general shopping in some quarters. The American Express Gold Card gives 4x at US supermarkets but only 1x at most retail stores.

A common approach many points collectors use is a "trifecta" style setup. One card for dining, one for groceries, one for everything else. For example, someone in the Chase ecosystem might pair the Sapphire Preferred (3x dining) with the Freedom Unlimited (1.5x everything else, or higher during promos). Someone in the Amex ecosystem might pair the Gold Card (4x dining, 4x US supermarkets) with a flat-rate cash back card for gas and general purchases.

The fix is simple. Write down where you actually spend money each month. Look at your last three statements. Then match categories to cards instead of grabbing whichever card is on top of your wallet.

## Am I Ignoring Caps on Bonus Categories?

Yes, many top dining and grocery cards cap how much bonus spending they will reward each year. Once you hit that cap, your rewards rate often drops hard, sometimes down to 1x.

This trips people up because the marketing headline never mentions the cap. You see "6% cash back at US supermarkets" and get excited. But the Blue Cash Preferred from American Express only gives that 6% rate on the first $6,000 spent per year at US supermarkets. After that, it drops to 1%.

The Chase Freedom Flex and Freedom Unlimited rotating or bonus categories often work in quarterly cycles too, usually capped near $1,500 in combined purchases per quarter for the 5% categories.

People get burned when they assume the high rate applies to all their spending all year. A family that spends $12,000 a year on groceries might expect big rewards, but half that spending falls outside the 6% cap and earns the base rate instead.

The fix is to actually check the cap before you commit to using a card as your main grocery or dining card. Some annual caps are generous enough to not matter for most households. Others are tight enough that you will blow through them by summer. If you are a big spender in one category, it sometimes makes sense to hold two cards that offer that same bonus category, so you have a second one to switch to once the first one caps out.

## Am I Forgetting That "Dining" Doesn't Always Mean What I Think?

Not always. Card issuers each define "dining" a little differently, and it matters for how you get rewarded. What counts as dining on one card might not count on another.

This is a quiet mistake because most people assume dining means restaurants and leave it at that. But definitions vary. American Express often counts food delivery services like Grubhub and Seamless as dining. Chase's dining category typically includes delivery services too, plus some fast food. Some cards also count bars, and some do not clearly say whether coffee shops always qualify.

There's also a wrinkle with merchant category codes. These are the codes point-of-sale systems use to tell your card issuer what kind of business you are shopping at. A restaurant inside a hotel or a casino sometimes codes differently than a standalone restaurant. A grocery store with a deli counter might code as a supermarket, or it might code as general merchandise, depending on their setup. This is why two people can swipe at the same type of place and get different rewards.

The fix is not to guess. Check your card issuer's dining category definition on their rewards terms page before assuming a purchase will earn bonus points. If a specific merchant is borderline, a quick search often turns up other cardholders reporting how it coded for them.

## Am I Chasing Sign-Up Bonuses Without Thinking About Long-Term Value?

This is a real risk if you are deep into churning cards. Sign-up bonuses are tempting, but a card's ongoing earning rate matters more if you plan to keep using it for years.

This mistake is common among people newer to the points hobby. A 60,000 or 75,000 point welcome offer looks huge compared to earning 3x or 4x day to day. So people chase the biggest bonus number without checking what happens after the bonus period ends.

Here's the thing. A welcome bonus is a one-time event. The daily earning rate is what actually builds your points balance over years of shopping and dining. A card offering 90,000 points but only 1x on dining afterward will fall behind a card offering 50,000 points with 3x or 4x ongoing dining rewards, if you keep the card long term.

This also connects directly to Chase 5/24. Every new card application uses up one of your five slots. If you grab a big bonus on a card that turns out to have a weak everyday earning rate, you've spent a valuable application slot on a card you might downgrade or stop using within a year.

A common approach is to think of applications in two buckets. Some cards you get purely for the bonus, then you may downgrade or stop using them once you've hit the spend requirement. Other cards you keep as a long-term "keeper" in your wallet because the ongoing rewards rate justifies carrying it for years. Knowing which bucket a card belongs to before you apply helps you use your limited 5/24 slots more wisely.

## Am I Overlooking Annual Fees Relative to My Actual Spending?

Yes, if you're comparing point values without doing basic math on your own spending habits first. A premium card with a high annual fee only makes sense if your spending in its bonus categories is high enough to outearn the fee.

This mistake happens because annual fees get buried under flashy welcome offer numbers and glossy card benefit lists. People see "$250 annual fee" and "4x on dining" and assume it's automatically worth it.

Do the math instead. Say a card has a $250 annual fee and gives 4x points on dining, where a no-fee card would give you 1x. That's a 3x point difference. If your points are worth roughly 1 cent each (a common, though not guaranteed, ballpark many people use for airline and hotel points), you'd need to spend about $8,300 a year on dining just to make up the fee difference in extra points. Below that spending level, the no-fee card might actually put more value in your pocket.

This doesn't mean premium cards are bad. Many carry credits, like dining credits, Uber credits, or annual travel credits that can offset the fee if you actually use them. But those credits only count if you remember to use them and they fit purchases you'd make anyway. A $10 monthly Uber Eats credit is worth $120 a year, but only if you actually order Uber Eats regularly.

The fix is to total up your annual dining and shopping spend, apply the bonus multiplier difference, and compare that against the annual fee minus any credits you'll reliably use. If the math doesn't clear the fee, a no-fee or lower-fee card is often the smarter pick.

## Am I Letting Cards Sit Unused and Wasting Their Value?

Yes, this is one of the sneakier mistakes. A card sitting in a drawer earns nothing, but it might still be costing you an annual fee, and it's still counting against your Chase 5/24 status.

This happens naturally over time. You get a card for a bonus, use it heavily for three months to hit the minimum spend, then move on to the next shiny offer. The old card gets forgotten. Meanwhile the annual fee renews every year whether you use the card or not.

There's also an opportunity cost angle specific to Chase 5/24. If a forgotten Chase card is sitting in your wallet, it's one of your five slots being used by a card you're not even getting value from anymore. Some people keep zombie cards around indefinitely without ever revisiting whether that slot could be better used.

The fix is a periodic checkup. Once or twice a year, pull up every card you have open. Ask three questions for each one. Is the annual fee justified by ongoing use or benefits? Is it still one of my best options for its bonus category? If it's a Chase card, is holding onto it worth the 5/24 slot compared to what else I could apply for?

If the answer is no across the board, options include downgrading the card to a no-fee version if the issuer allows it, or closing it outright. Downgrading is often gentler on your credit history than closing, since it keeps the account and its age intact.

## Putting It Together

There's no universal winner for "best card for shopping and dining." The mistakes above all come from the same root cause: treating card selection as a one-time decision instead of an ongoing strategy. Categories change, caps reset, spending habits shift, and new cards launch. A setup that made sense two years ago might not make sense today. Revisiting your lineup regularly, and matching cards to your real spending rather than the flashiest headline offer, is what actually separates strong points earners from people who leave value on the table.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best credit card for shopping and dining rewards?
The Chase Sabre Preferred Card (or similar top-tier travel rewards card) is often considered the best option, offering elevated points on dining and select shopping categories. It typically earns 3x points on dining, including takeout and delivery, plus bonus categories that rotate or include online shopping platforms. Its strong sign-up bonus and flexible point redemption through transfer partners make it especially valuable for cardholders who spend heavily in these categories and want to maximize travel value from everyday purchases.

### How does the Chase 5/24 rule affect getting a shopping and dining rewards card?
The 5/24 rule means Chase will likely deny your application if you've opened five or more credit cards (from any issuer) in the past 24 months. This applies to nearly all Chase consumer cards, including top dining and shopping rewards options. Before applying, check your recent account openings across all issuers. If you're close to or over the limit, consider applying for Chase cards first before pursuing cards from other banks that don't have similar restrictions.

### What sign-up bonus can I expect from a dining and shopping credit card?
Most premium shopping and dining cards offer bonuses ranging from 60,000 to 100,000 points after meeting a minimum spending requirement, usually $4,000–$6,000 within three months. These bonuses can be worth $600–$1,500 or more depending on redemption method, especially when transferred to airline or hotel partners. Always confirm current offers directly, as sign-up bonuses fluctuate frequently and may include limited-time elevated promotions worth pursuing strategically.

### Should I get a card with rotating categories or fixed dining/shopping rewards?
Fixed-rate rewards cards are generally better for consistent shopping and dining spenders since they don't require activation or category tracking. Rotating category cards can offer higher percentages (like 5%) but only during specific quarters and often with spending caps. If your dining and shopping habits are steady throughout the year, a fixed 3x or 4x points card provides more reliable, hassle-free value than chasing quarterly promotions.

### How many credit cards should I apply for to maximize dining and shopping rewards?
Most experts recommend applying for one to two new cards at a time, spaced three to six months apart, to protect your credit score and stay within issuer-specific rules like Chase's 5/24. This approach lets you meet sign-up bonus spending requirements comfortably while monitoring your credit utilization and inquiries. A strategic, gradual approach ensures long-term approval odds while still allowing you to build a strong rewards-earning card portfolio.

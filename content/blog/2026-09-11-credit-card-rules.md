# Bank "Rules" That Aren't Written Down Anywhere: Which Ones Actually Matter?

Chase 5/24 gets all the attention because Chase publishes hints about it. But other banks run their own quiet rules. This guide compares the big unwritten rules from Amex, Citi, Bank of America, Capital One, and US Bank, so you can figure out which ones actually affect your next application.

## What are the major unwritten card rules besides Chase 5/24?

Every major bank has some kind of velocity or eligibility rule that isn't in any official document. Amex has once-per-lifetime bonus limits and an application velocity watch. Citi has an 8/65 rule and a 48-month bonus rule. Bank of America has a 2/3/4 rule. Capital One and US Bank run their own softer versions.

These rules got their names from the community, not the banks. People noticed patterns in denials and approvals, then gave them nicknames. Some are well documented with years of data behind them. Others are shakier and based on fewer reports. Knowing the difference matters when you're planning which card to apply for next.

## What is Amex's once-per-lifetime rule and does it still apply?

Amex limits most personal cards to one welcome bonus per card per lifetime. If you already got the bonus on a specific card, you generally won't get it again, even years later. This has been true for a long time and shows no sign of changing.

This rule applies to the exact card, not the whole brand. If you had the Amex Gold Card and got the bonus in 2015, you're usually not eligible for that same bonus again. But if Amex later renames or restructures a card into a meaningfully different product, some people have found new eligibility. This is inconsistent and not something to count on.

Business cards from Amex have historically been treated more loosely than personal cards. Some cardholders have reported getting a business card bonus more than once over many years. This isn't guaranteed and Amex could tighten it at any time. A common approach is to treat every welcome bonus as a one-time event and plan around cards you haven't held before.

## What is Amex's application velocity rule?

Amex doesn't publish a specific number, but many applicants report trouble getting approved if they've opened several new accounts in a short window. This is often called the "under 5/24 for Amex" idea informally, though Amex has never confirmed a number like Chase has.

The general pattern people report is caution around opening more than one or two Amex cards in a rolling two-year period, especially if you're also opening cards from other banks at the same time. Amex also watches your total number of open Amex accounts. Having a lot of open cards, even old ones, can sometimes affect approval odds for a new one.

Amex also enforces a 2 card per 90 days and 5 card per 65 days type limit on applications, though the exact windows shift over time and aren't officially published. If you get denied for "too many recent inquiries" or "too many new accounts," this is usually why.

## What is Citi's 8/65 rule?

Citi generally won't approve you for a new personal card if you've opened 2 or more new cards from any bank in the last 65 days, or 8 or more in the last 24 months. This rule is separate from Chase 5/24 and applies on top of it.

The 8/65 rule counts cards from every bank, not just Citi. So if you opened a Chase card, an Amex card, and a Capital One card in the last two months, that could already put you over the 65-day limit for a new Citi application. This rule is well documented and has held up consistently for years, making it one of the more reliable rules outside of Chase's own.

Citi also runs a separate rule limiting how often you can get the same card's bonus. Historically this was a 24-month rule, then it moved toward a 48-month rule for many cards. The exact window has changed more than once, so it's worth checking current terms on the card's application page before assuming when you'd be eligible again for a bonus you already received.

## What is Bank of America's 2/3/4 rule?

Bank of America has an informal limit often described as 2 new cards in 2 months, 3 in 12 months, and 4 in 24 months. This applies across all banks, not just Bank of America cards, similar to how Chase 5/24 counts every card you've opened.

This rule is less rigid than Chase 5/24. Many people report approvals even when slightly over these numbers, especially if they have a strong existing relationship with Bank of America, like a checking account or a mortgage. Relationship banking seems to carry real weight here in a way it doesn't with some other issuers.

Because this rule is softer and less consistent, treat it as a rough guideline rather than a hard cutoff. If you're well under the numbers, you're probably fine. If you're right at the edge, results seem to vary more than they do with Citi's 8/65 rule.

## Does Capital One have an application rule like this?

Capital One doesn't have a widely confirmed numeric rule like Citi or Bank of America. Instead, people report general caution around recent hard inquiries and recently opened accounts, without a specific published threshold.

Capital One is known for pulling all three credit bureau reports on a single application, which is unusual. Denials often come down to overall credit profile rather than a specific card-count rule. Some cardholders also report a one bonus per card lifetime pattern here too, similar to Amex, though it's less consistently documented across the community.

Because the data here is thinner than for Chase, Citi, or Bank of America, it's harder to give a confident numeric guideline. A cautious approach is to avoid applying for a Capital One card right after a flurry of other new accounts, even without a confirmed rule forcing that caution.

## What about US Bank?

US Bank runs a version of a velocity rule sometimes described loosely, with people reporting denials tied to recent account openings across all banks, similar in spirit to Bank of America's approach. There's no widely agreed specific number the way there is for Citi.

US Bank also has a reputation for weighing your existing relationship with them, similar to Bank of America. Having a checking or savings account, or an existing card in good standing, seems to help. This isn't guaranteed to change an outcome, but it's a pattern many in the community report.

## Side-by-side comparison

Here's how the major rules stack up against each other.

**Chase 5/24**: 5 or more new cards from any bank in 24 months usually blocks approval. Well documented, very consistent, official-ish.

**Amex once-per-lifetime**: One bonus per exact card, per lifetime, for personal cards. Very well documented, extremely consistent.

**Amex velocity**: Roughly 2 cards per 90 days, 5 per 65 days type limits, unofficial and shifting. Moderately documented.

**Citi 8/65**: 2 or more cards in 65 days, or 8 or more in 24 months, blocks new Citi approval. Well documented, fairly consistent.

**Citi bonus repeat rule**: Historically 24 months, now often 48 months for the same card's bonus. Check current terms, this has changed more than once.

**Bank of America 2/3/4**: 2 cards in 2 months, 3 in 12 months, 4 in 24 months, loosely enforced. Moderately documented, softer than Chase or Citi.

**Capital One**: No confirmed numeric rule. Pulls all three bureau reports. Thin documentation.

**US Bank**: Loose velocity pattern, relationship banking seems to help. Thin documentation.

## Which rule should you actually plan around?

For most people building a points strategy, Chase 5/24 and Citi's 8/65 rule matter the most because they're the most consistent and predictable. Amex's once-per-lifetime rule matters most when deciding whether a specific card is even worth applying for again.

If your main goal is Chase cards, your whole plan should start with your 5/24 count. Once you know how many slots you have before hitting 5, you can decide whether to prioritize Chase cards first or fit in other bank cards around them.

If you're also eyeing Citi cards, watch the 65-day window closely. Opening two cards from other banks in quick succession, then applying for a Citi card a few weeks later, is a common way people accidentally trigger a denial they didn't expect.

For Amex, the planning question is different. It's less about timing and more about whether you've already gotten that specific card's bonus before. Many people find it useful to keep a simple list of every card they've held and when, specifically so they don't waste an application on a bonus they already collected.

Bank of America, Capital One, and US Bank rules matter less for sequencing and more as a general caution. If you've opened several cards recently across any bank, it might be worth spacing out your next application by a month or two before trying one of these issuers, even without a hard rule forcing your hand.

## How do you build an application order around all of this?

A common approach is to sequence applications by rule strictness, hitting the least forgiving rules first. That usually means Chase 5/24 sensitive cards early, since every new card anywhere counts against that number permanently for 24 months.

After Chase, many people slot in Citi cards, being careful to space them at least 65 days from any other new account. Amex cards can often go anywhere in the sequence, since the main constraint is bonus history rather than timing, though respecting some space between Amex applications is still wise given the velocity concerns.

Bank of America, Capital One, and US Bank are usually good candidates for the middle or end of a sequence, once you already know your recent account-opening pace is on the slower side. This isn't a guarantee of approval with any issuer. It's simply a way to stack the odds in a sensible order based on which rules are strictest and best documented.

Track your own applications somewhere simple, a spreadsheet works fine. Note the bank, the card, the date, and whether you got a bonus. That single habit makes every one of these rules easier to plan around, because you'll always know exactly where you stand before you apply.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the Chase 5/24 rule?
The Chase 5/24 rule means Chase will typically deny your application for a new credit card if you've opened 5 or more personal credit cards (from any bank) within the past 24 months. This unwritten policy applies to most Chase consumer cards, including popular travel rewards cards like the Sapphire Preferred and Freedom Unlimited. Business cards from other banks often count toward your total, though Chase's own business cards usually don't. Tracking your recent openings carefully before applying can save you an unnecessary hard inquiry and denial.

### How do I know if I'm under 5/24?
Count every personal credit card you've opened in the last 24 months, including authorized user accounts in some cases, then check if that total is below 5. Business cards from issuers other than Chase (like Amex or Capital One business cards) typically count too, since they report to your personal credit file. Store cards and retail credit cards usually count as well. Pull your credit reports from all three bureaus to get an accurate, complete picture before applying for a Chase card.

### Which cards don't count toward 5/24?
Chase's own business cards, like the Ink Business Preferred, generally don't count toward your 5/24 total, along with authorized user cards in many cases and charge cards without a preset spending limit from Amex. Store or retail cards sometimes don't report as "credit cards" depending on the issuer, so they may not count either. However, policies can change, so always verify current terms before applying. When in doubt, assume a card counts unless you've confirmed otherwise through recent data points from other applicants.

### What order should I apply for credit cards to maximize sign-up bonuses?
Apply for Chase cards first, before you hit 5/24, since Chase is the most restrictive issuer regarding recent account openings. Once you've secured the Chase cards you want, move to issuers with more flexible rules like Amex, Citi, or Capital One, which have their own separate restrictions like Amex's once-per-lifetime bonus rule. Spacing applications 3-6 months apart also helps manage credit score impact and approval odds, since issuers scrutinize recent inquiries and new accounts closely.

### Can I still get approved for a card if I'm over 5/24?
Yes, but only for cards not subject to the 5/24 rule, since being over the limit will almost certainly get you denied for restricted Chase consumer cards. Many Chase business cards, along with cards from Amex, Citi, Capital One, and other issuers, don't check your 5/24 status at all. Focus your applications on these alternatives while you wait for older accounts to age past the 24-month mark, which will eventually bring your count back under 5.

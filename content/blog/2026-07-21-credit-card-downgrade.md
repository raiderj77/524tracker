# Credit Card Downgrade: When It Helps Your 5/24 Game and When It Doesn't

Downgrading a card keeps the account open but swaps it for a no-fee or lower-fee version. It does not remove the card from your 5/24 count. If your goal is a lower 5/24 count, downgrading won't do it. But it can still save you money and protect your credit age.

## Does Downgrading Help My 5/24 Count?

No, downgrading does not help your 5/24 count. Chase tracks the account open date rather than the current card type, so a downgrade from Sapphire Preferred to Freedom Unlimited still counts as an original open date.


No. Downgrading does not help your 5/24 count at all. Chase counts every personal card opened in the last 24 months, no matter what happens to it later.

Here's the thing that trips people up. Chase looks at the account open date, not the current product name. If you opened a Chase Sapphire Preferred 15 months ago and downgrade it today to a Chase Freedom Unlimited, that account still shows as opened 15 months ago. It still counts toward your 5/24 total.

The only things that remove a card from your 5/24 count are:

- The account ages past 24 months
- You close the account and enough time passes (closing doesn't speed this up either)

So if you're staring down your 5/24 count and hoping a downgrade will buy you room for a new application, it won't. You need to wait out the clock. Downgrading and closing both leave the open date exactly where it was.

## Then Why Do People Downgrade at All?

People downgrade to stop paying an annual fee on a card they no longer use enough to justify. It also keeps the account open, which protects your average age of accounts and keeps a credit line active.

Say you got the Chase Sapphire Reserve for the airport lounge access and the big travel credits. Two years later, you're not traveling as much. The $550 or $795 annual fee (Chase has raised this fee over time, so check the current number) stops making sense. You have three choices: keep paying, close it, or downgrade it.

Downgrading to something like the Chase Freedom Unlimited or Chase Freedom Flex means:

- No more annual fee
- The account stays open
- Your credit history on that account keeps building
- Your credit limit on that line stays in place, which helps your overall utilization

Closing the card would also stop the fee. But closing has its own downsides, which we'll get into.

## What Are My Actual Options Here?

You generally have four paths: keep the card and pay the fee, downgrade to a no-fee version, product change to a different fee-based card, or close the account. Each one trades something for something else.

Let's lay them out side by side.

**Option 1: Keep paying the annual fee**

This makes sense if you're actually using the perks. A $95 annual fee card that gives you a $50 credit and extra points on a category you spend a lot in can still be worth it. Many people find that running the math once a year, right before the fee hits, is a good habit. Add up what you actually used. Compare it to the fee. If the card wins, keep it.

**Option 2: Downgrade to a no-fee card**

This is the move when you like having the account open but the fee no longer pencils out. Common downgrade paths inside Chase include:

- Sapphire Reserve or Sapphire Preferred down to Freedom Unlimited or Freedom Flex
- Ink Business Preferred down to Ink Business Cash or Ink Business Unlimited
- World of Hyatt card down to... actually, Hyatt has no no-fee downgrade option within Chase, which matters later

You keep the credit line, the account age, and often keep any card-specific perks tied to just having the account, though you lose the premium benefits like lounge access or travel credits.

**Option 3: Product change to a different fee card**

Sometimes you don't want the no-fee version, you want a different fee tier. Chase sometimes lets you move from the Sapphire Reserve to the Sapphire Preferred instead of jumping straight to a free card. This lowers your fee without dropping it to zero, and you might keep a middle tier of benefits.

**Option 4: Close the account**

This stops the fee completely and removes the credit line from your utilization math (which can actually hurt your utilization if that was a big chunk of your available credit). It does not help your 5/24 count. It can shorten your average account age over time, especially if it was one of your older cards.

## How Do I Know Which One Is Right for Me?

Pick based on three things: whether you still get value from the perks, how much you care about the account's age and history, and whether you plan to reapply for that same card family later. A common approach is to downgrade rather than close, unless the card has no downgrade path.

Walk through these questions:

**Am I using the perks enough to cover the fee?**

If yes, keep the card as is. If no, move to the next question.

**Does this card have a no-fee downgrade option?**

Not every card does. The Chase Sapphire Reserve and Sapphire Preferred downgrade cleanly to Freedom products. Chase Ink Business Preferred downgrades to Ink Business Cash or Unlimited. But co-branded cards, like the World of Hyatt Credit Card or many airline cards, often have no true no-fee version within that issuer relationship. For those, your real choice is closing or eating the fee.

**How much do I care about this account's age?**

If this is one of your oldest cards, closing it will eventually shrink your average account age. That can matter for your credit score, though it's one factor among many. Downgrading avoids this entirely since the account stays open and active.

**Do I want to hold this card family again someday?**

This is the part that trips up a lot of people in the points hobby. Some card bonuses have rules about how often you can earn them again. If you close a Sapphire card and want the bonus again later, you may run into Chase's rule that you generally cannot receive a Sapphire bonus if you've received one in the past 48 months, and you cannot hold both Sapphire Preferred and Sapphire Reserve at the same time in some cases. Downgrading, then upgrading back later, sometimes works differently than closing and reapplying cold. Rules here shift, so check current terms before assuming either path locks you out or clears you.

## What Happens to My Points If I Downgrade?

Your points usually stay put as long as you keep a Chase card open that can hold them. But if you downgrade your only premium Chase card to a card that earns a different currency, you may lose the ability to transfer those points to airline and hotel partners.

This is the part people forget until it's too late. Chase Ultimate Rewards points earned on a Sapphire card get access to transfer partners like United, Hyatt, and Southwest. Points earned on a plain Freedom card do not have transfer access on their own.

If you're holding a big pile of transferable points and you downgrade your Sapphire card without having another Sapphire, Ink Preferred, or Business Preferred card open, your existing points don't disappear, but you may lose the option to transfer new points to travel partners going forward, and in some cases the ability to transfer the pile you already have. The safe move, if you carry a points balance and want to keep transfer options open, is to downgrade only after confirming you still hold at least one card in your account that keeps transfer partner access active.

Before downgrading a card that unlocks transfer partners, a common approach is to transfer out any points you plan to use, or confirm another qualifying card is still open on the account.

## Does Downgrading Trigger a New Bonus Clock?

No. Downgrading is not the same as applying for a new card, so it does not start a new bonus-eligibility clock the way opening a fresh account does.

But it can affect whether you're eligible for that card's bonus again later. Many Chase bonuses have language like "we may not award this bonus if you have received a new cardmember bonus for this same card in the past 24 months," or the previously mentioned 48-month Sapphire rule. Downgrading itself usually doesn't reset or restart these windows in your favor. It mostly just stops the fee while leaving the account and its history in place.

## What About Business Cards?

Business card downgrades work about the same way, but with a few extra wrinkles worth knowing.

Business cards don't show up on your personal credit report with most banks, Chase included, which means they generally don't count against your personal 5/24 total in the first place (opening one still might, depending on how the issuer reports, so this varies). Downgrading a business card doesn't change that. If you downgrade an Ink Business Preferred to an Ink Business Unlimited, the account stays open under your business, keeps its age, and drops the fee. Same logic as personal cards, just on the business side of your credit file.

## Quick Decision Checklist

Run through this before your renewal date hits:

1. Add up the value you got from the card's perks this year.
2. Compare that number to the annual fee.
3. If the fee wins, check whether a no-fee downgrade path exists.
4. If it exists, check whether downgrading affects your points' transfer access.
5. If you still want travel transfer options, confirm another qualifying card stays open.
6. If no downgrade path exists, weigh closing against just paying the fee one more year.
7. Remember none of this moves your 5/24 count either way.

## The Bottom Line

Downgrading is a tool for managing fees and protecting account age, not a tool for fixing your 5/24 count. If your card still earns its fee back in value, keep it. If it doesn't, and a no-fee version exists, downgrading is usually the gentler move compared to closing. Just check your points situation first, since some downgrades quietly cut off transfer partner access if you're not paying attention.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does downgrading a credit card affect Chase 5/24 status?
No, downgrading a credit card does not remove it from your Chase 5/24 count. The 5/24 rule counts any new credit card account opened in the past 24 months, regardless of what happens to it afterward. Once a card is opened, it stays on your 5/24 tally until it "ages off" 24 months from the original open date, whether you keep it, downgrade it, or even close it. Downgrading only changes the product and its ongoing benefits, not the account's opening date used for 5/24 calculations.

### Should I downgrade or cancel a credit card before applying for a new one?
Neither action helps your 5/24 count, so decide based on annual fees and card value instead. Since downgrading or closing a card doesn't remove it from your 5/24 total, there's no rush to do either before applying. If a card charges an annual fee you no longer find worthwhile, downgrading to a no-fee version preserves your credit history and average account age while eliminating the cost. Canceling can shorten your credit history and lower available credit, so downgrading is usually the safer choice when you're unsure.

### What happens to my sign-up bonus if I downgrade a card?
You keep any sign-up bonus you already earned when you downgrade a card. Downgrading doesn't claw back rewards you've received, since the bonus was earned by meeting the spending requirement, not by keeping the card long-term. However, you should downgrade only after the bonus posts and any related annual fee has been charged, since some issuers won't refund fees if you downgrade too early. Always confirm your points have posted to your account before initiating a downgrade to avoid losing pending rewards.

### Can I get another sign-up bonus if I downgrade and later reapply for the same card?
Usually yes, but timing depends on the issuer's specific bonus eligibility rules. Chase, for example, generally requires you not to have earned a bonus on that same card in the past 48 months, and some cards have "once per lifetime" restrictions. Downgrading itself doesn't reset the bonus clock, it's the product and issuer-specific rule that matters. Before reapplying, check the current terms for that exact card, since Chase and other issuers frequently update eligibility language for sign-up bonuses.

### When is the best time to downgrade a credit card instead of closing it?
The best time is right before your annual fee posts, if you've already gotten the value you wanted from the card. Downgrading at this point avoids paying another year's fee while preserving the account's age and credit line, which helps your credit utilization and average account age. Keep in mind the card stays on your 5/24 report either way, so downgrading offers no strategic benefit for future approvals, it's purely a cost-saving and credit-health move, not a 5/24 workaround.

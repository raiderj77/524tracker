# Which Credit Card Is Best for All Purpose Spending?

There's no single best card for everyone. For most people building a points strategy, a flat 2% cash back card or a flexible points card like the Chase Sabre Preferred or Capital One Venture works best as a catch-all. The right pick depends on which bank ecosystem you're already in and what you do with your points.

## What Does "All Purpose" Even Mean?

An all-purpose card is the one you use for spending that doesn't fit a bonus category. It's your default swipe. This matters because most cards only earn extra points on specific things like groceries or gas.

Think about your monthly spending. Rent, insurance, random Amazon orders, doctor visits, that random Target run. None of that fits neatly into "dining" or "travel." You need a card that earns something decent no matter what you buy.

The goal is to pick a card that earns a solid flat rate, or one that earns points you can actually use well. Some people care more about simplicity. Others care more about squeezing out maximum value. Both are valid approaches.

## Should I Use a Flat-Rate Cash Back Card?

Yes, if you want simple and predictable rewards without much thought. Cards like the Citi Double Cash and Wells Fargo Active Cash earn 2% on everything, no categories to track. This is the easiest option and a common starting point for many people.

The Citi Double Cash earns 1% when you buy something and another 1% when you pay it off. That structure rewards paying your bill, which is good financial behavior anyway. Wells Fargo Active Cash also earns a flat 2% with no annual fee.

These cards are hard to beat for pure simplicity. You don't need a spreadsheet. You don't need to remember which card gets 3x on office supply stores this quarter. You swipe, you earn 2%, done.

The tradeoff is that cash back is cash back. A cent is a cent. There's no chance to stretch that value further through transfer partners or travel redemptions. If you're the kind of person who wants to optimize every point, a flat cash back card will feel like leaving value on the table.

## Should I Use a Flexible Points Card Instead?

Yes, if you're willing to put in a little more effort for potentially higher value. Cards like the Chase Sapphire Preferred, Capital One Venture, and American Express Gold all earn points that can be worth more than one cent each, especially when transferred to airline or hotel partners.

The Chase Sapphire Preferred earns 1x on general purchases but boosts to higher multipliers on travel and dining. Chase Ultimate Rewards points can transfer to partners like United, Hyatt, and Southwest. Many hobbyists find that transferring points to the right partner can unlock more value than a flat 2% redemption, though this depends on the specific transfer and how the points are used.

Capital One Venture earns a flat 2x miles on everything, which makes it a strong all-purpose pick if you don't want to think about categories but still want access to Capital One's transfer partners, like Air Canada Aeroplan or Turkish Airlines Miles&Smiles.

American Express Membership Rewards, earned through cards like the Amex Gold or Amex Green, also transfer to a wide range of airline partners. The Gold card earns 4x at restaurants and 4x at U.S. supermarkets (on the first $25,000 per year), then 1x on everything else. So while it shines in specific categories, it's less ideal as a true all-purpose card compared to something like the Venture.

The tradeoff with flexible points is that their value isn't fixed. A point might be worth 1.5 cents when transferred well, or barely 1 cent if redeemed poorly through a travel portal. This makes them better suited to people who enjoy researching redemptions.

## What About a Card Already Tied to My Ecosystem?

This is often the smartest move, even though it's the most overlooked. If you already hold cards in the Chase or Amex ecosystem, adding a strong flat-rate earner in that same system can make your points easier to combine and use.

Here's why this matters. Chase Ultimate Rewards points from a no-annual-fee card like the Chase Freedom Unlimited (earning 1.5x on everything) can be combined with points from a Sapphire Preferred or Sapphire Reserve. Once combined, they unlock transfer partners that the Freedom Unlimited alone doesn't offer.

So a common approach is to hold a Freedom Unlimited as the everyday spender, and a Sapphire card as the "point booster" that unlocks transfers. This lets you earn a strong flat rate on everyday purchases while still accessing airline and hotel transfer partners.

The same logic applies to Amex. The Amex Blue Business Plus, for business owners, earns 2x Membership Rewards points on the first $50,000 spent each year, with no annual fee. Paired with a card that has transfer partners, like the Amex Gold or Platinum, this becomes a strong all-purpose combo.

If you're already deep into one ecosystem, staying inside it for your all-purpose card often makes more sense than branching out to a new one.

## Does the Annual Fee Matter for an All-Purpose Card?

Yes, especially since this is the card you'll use constantly. A high annual fee needs to be justified by either strong earning rates or valuable perks you'll actually use.

The Citi Double Cash and Wells Fargo Active Cash have no annual fee, which makes the math simple. Every dollar you earn is pure profit against the card's cost.

The Capital One Venture has a $95 annual fee. To make that worth it over a no-fee 2% card, you need to get more than 2 cents of value per dollar spent, on average, from your redemptions. Many cardholders manage this through transfer partners, but it does take some effort.

The Chase Sapphire Preferred has a $95 annual fee too, but it comes with a $50 annual hotel credit through the Chase travel portal, which can offset a good chunk of the fee for people who book hotels that way.

Higher-fee cards like the Chase Sapphire Reserve ($550 annual fee) or Amex Platinum ($695 annual fee) come loaded with credits for things like TSA PreCheck, lounge access, and travel credits. These can be worth it, but only if you actually use those specific perks. A lounge you never visit isn't worth anything.

Before picking your all-purpose card, add up what you actually use in a year. If a card's perks don't match your real habits, a simpler no-fee option is often the smarter and less stressful choice.

## How Does This Fit Into My Chase 5/24 Strategy?

Your all-purpose card choice should factor into your card sequencing under 5/24. Since Chase cards count against your five-card limit regardless of issuer, many people choose to apply for Chase products first, then fill in with Amex, Capital One, or Citi cards for their all-purpose spending needs.

If you're still under 5/24, a Chase Sapphire Preferred or Chase Freedom Unlimited might make sense to grab now, while you still qualify. These cards don't just serve as good all-purpose earners. They also set up your Ultimate Rewards ecosystem for future transfers.

If you're already over 5/24, that's not a dead end. Amex and Capital One don't follow Chase's 5/24 rule for approval, which is why cards like the Amex Gold, Amex Green, or Capital One Venture become attractive all-purpose options once Chase is off the table. You can still build a strong points strategy, just through a different ecosystem.

This is one reason many hobbyists suggest applying for Chase cards earlier in a credit card journey, since Chase becomes harder to add once you're past five new accounts in 24 months. Your all-purpose card pick is a good moment to think through this sequencing, not just pick whatever card has the flashiest sign-up bonus.

## So Which One Should I Actually Pick?

If you want zero complexity, pick a flat 2% cash back card like the Citi Double Cash or Wells Fargo Active Cash. If you want more value potential and don't mind some research, pick a flexible points card like the Capital One Venture or a Chase Ultimate Rewards earner.

Ask yourself these questions before deciding:

**Do I already hold cards in a specific rewards ecosystem?** If yes, staying in that ecosystem usually makes your points easier to combine and use well.

**Am I under 5/24 right now?** If yes, this might be the moment to prioritize a Chase card before that door closes.

**Do I actually redeem points for travel, or do I just want simple cash back?** Be honest here. If you've never transferred a point to an airline partner, a flexible points card might not be worth the extra fee or complexity.

**How much do I spend on categories that don't get bonus points?** If it's a large chunk of your budget, your all-purpose card matters more than your category cards.

There's no universally correct answer. A points-curious beginner might do best starting with a simple no-fee 2% card to build good habits. A deep-in-the-hobby optimizer might run three or four cards, each covering a different spending gap, with a flexible points card as the fallback for everything else.

Pick based on your actual behavior, not the theoretical best card on paper. The best all-purpose card is the one that matches how you already spend and redeem, not the one with the biggest headline number.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the single best all-purpose credit card for everyday spending?
The Chase Sapphire Preferred is widely considered the best all-purpose credit card. It earns 2x points on all travel and dining, with a strong sign-up bonus and flexible points that transfer to over a dozen airline and hotel partners. Beyond category bonuses, it offers a 25% redemption boost through Chase Travel and no foreign transaction fees. Because Chase points hold outsized value compared to cash-back equivalents, this card strikes the best balance of flexibility, earning power, and reasonable annual fee for someone wanting one card to cover most purchases.

### How does the Chase 5/24 rule affect which card I should get first?
The 5/24 rule means Chase denies most credit card applications if you've opened five or more personal cards (any bank) in the past 24 months. Since Chase cards like the Sapphire Preferred and Ink Business cards offer some of the best all-purpose value, you should apply for them early, before opening cards from other issuers. Prioritizing Chase first protects your ability to earn premium points and preserves flexibility, since non-Chase cards won't block future Chase approvals the way Chase applications count against your 5/24 status.

### Is an annual fee credit card worth it for all-purpose use?
Yes, if you spend enough to offset the fee through rewards and benefits. Cards like the Chase Sapphire Preferred ($95) or Capital One Venture X ($395) offer travel credits, points multipliers, and transfer partners that often exceed the fee's cost within normal spending. For light spenders, a no-annual-fee card like the Chase Freedom Unlimited may be more practical. The right choice depends on whether you'll actually use included perks like travel credits, lounge access, or bonus categories enough to justify the yearly cost.

### Should I choose a cash-back card or a points/miles card for general spending?
It depends on your goals: cash back is simpler, while points and miles offer higher potential value if redeemed strategically. Cash-back cards like the Citi Double Cash give a flat, guaranteed 2% return with no redemption complexity. Points cards like the Chase Sapphire Preferred can be worth 1.5–2x more per point when transferred to travel partners, but require more research and effort. Choose cash back for simplicity and predictability, or points/miles if you're willing to learn redemption strategies for outsized travel value.

### How many credit cards should I apply for to maximize sign-up bonuses without hurting my credit?
Most people should space out applications by 3–6 months and stay mindful of issuer-specific rules like Chase's 5/24. Applying too quickly can trigger denials, hard inquiries that temporarily lower your score, and issuer scrutiny (such as Chase's 48-month rule on the Sapphire bonus). A sustainable strategy is applying for one or two well-chosen cards at a time, focusing on high-value bonuses first, and monitoring your total new accounts within a 24-month window to preserve future approval odds.

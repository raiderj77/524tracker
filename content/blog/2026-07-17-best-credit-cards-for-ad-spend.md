# Best Credit Cards for Ad Spend: Common Mistakes to Avoid

Ad spend can be a fast way to hit sign-up bonuses, but it also causes real damage if you pick the wrong card or run it the wrong way. The biggest mistakes are choosing cards with low ad bonus categories, ignoring 5/24 impact, and forgetting how ad spend affects your issuer relationship long term.

## Am I Picking the Wrong Card for Ad Spend Categories?

Yes, if you grab a card just because it has a big sign-up bonus and skip checking the bonus category rules. Many ad platforms code as "advertising" or "business services," and not every card rewards that category well.

This mistake happens because people get excited about a 100,000-point offer and stop reading. They assume all business cards reward ad spend the same way. They don't.

Some cards give bonus points on advertising purchases made directly with select platforms, like Facebook or Google Ads. Others only bonus categories like office supplies, shipping, or travel. If your card's bonus categories don't include advertising, you'll earn base rate, often just 1 point per dollar, on what might be your biggest spend category all year.

A common approach is to check the issuer's official bonus category list before applying, not after. Search the exact card name plus "bonus categories" and look at the issuer's own page. Category coding also depends on how the ad platform itself is set up, not just your card. A charge from Google Ads might code differently than one from a smaller ad network, even on the same card.

To avoid this mistake:

- Confirm advertising is a listed bonus category, not just assumed.
- Check if there's a spending cap on the bonus category. Some cards cap bonus earning at $50,000 per year combined across categories, then drop to 1 point per dollar after that.
- Look for real user reports on how specific ad platforms coded, since issuer category lists don't always match reality.

## Am I Ignoring How Ad Spend Hits My 5/24 Count?

Every new card you open for ad spend still counts toward your 5/24 total, even business cards from some issuers. This trips people up because they treat ad spend cards as a separate track from their personal card strategy.

The mistake happens because ad spend is often tied to a business, and people assume business cards don't count against 5/24. That's true for Chase's own business cards in many cases, since Chase doesn't report most of its business cards to your personal credit report. But cards from other issuers still show up. American Express, Capital One, and others often do report business cards to personal credit, and those will count toward your 5/24 total even though they're for business use.

This matters a lot if you're planning to apply for a Chase Sapphire card or a Chase co-branded airline card later. If you open three non-Chase business cards for ad spend bonuses, you may have burned through part of your 5/24 window before you ever apply for the Chase card you actually wanted.

A common approach is to map out your target Chase cards first, then plan ad spend cards around that timeline. If a Chase card is high on your list, many people try to open it before opening cards from issuers that report to their personal credit.

To avoid this mistake:

- Check whether the ad spend card you want reports to your personal credit report or your business credit report.
- Order your applications so Chase cards come before cards that will add to your 5/24 count, if a Chase card is a priority.
- Track your 5/24 count with a spreadsheet or a tracking tool, updating it every time you apply, not just when you get approved.

## Am I Overestimating My Real Ad Spend Volume?

People often guess high when estimating monthly ad spend, then pick a card with a bonus that's out of reach. This leads to disappointment and sometimes to overspending just to chase a bonus.

This mistake happens because ad agencies and marketers see spend numbers change month to month. A client might cut their budget, pause a campaign, or shift platforms. If you signed up for a card expecting $15,000 a month in ad spend to hit a bonus, but your actual average is closer to $8,000, you might miss the minimum spend window entirely.

A common approach is to look at your last six months of actual ad spend, not your best month ever. Average it out. Then compare that average against the minimum spend requirement and the time window given, usually three to six months for most current offers.

For example, if a card requires $15,000 in spend within three months, that's $5,000 a month. If your real average is $6,000 a month, you have some cushion. If your real average is $4,000, you're already behind before you start.

To avoid this mistake:

- Pull real numbers from past ad platform invoices before applying, not a rough guess.
- Build in a buffer for slow months, since ad budgets often shrink around holidays or end of quarter.
- Avoid stacking multiple new cards with high minimum spends in the same three-month window unless your ad spend is very high and consistent.

## Am I Running Ad Spend Through Personal Cards by Habit?

A lot of people default to running client ad spend through personal cards because that's the card already saved in their ad platform account. This blurs personal and business spend and can cause problems later.

This mistake happens out of convenience. The personal card is already linked to the ad account. Adding a new card means updating billing info, which feels like a hassle. So people keep charging tens of thousands of dollars a month in client ad spend to a personal card meant for groceries and gas.

The problem shows up in a few ways. First, utilization on that personal card can spike hard. If you have a $10,000 limit and run $8,000 in ad spend before paying it off, your reported utilization jumps, which can lower your credit score right when you might want to apply for another card. Second, mixing business and personal spend makes bookkeeping harder, especially at tax time. Third, some issuers may flag unusual spend patterns on personal cards used for business-level volume, though this varies by issuer and account history.

A common approach is to open a dedicated business card for ad spend, even a no-annual-fee option, just to separate the transactions. Many people also request a credit limit increase specifically because they know ad spend volume will be high, rather than letting utilization spike unexpectedly.

To avoid this mistake:

- Use a separate card, ideally a business card, for any recurring client or agency ad spend.
- Ask for a starting credit limit that matches your expected monthly ad spend, not just your personal spending habits.
- Pay down balances mid-cycle if you're running high volume, rather than waiting for the statement to close, to keep reported utilization lower.

## Am I Forgetting That Ad Networks Sometimes Hold or Reverse Charges?

Ad platforms occasionally place holds, reverse charges, or pause ad accounts for review, and this can mess with your minimum spend timeline. People often forget this risk exists until it happens to them.

This mistake happens because most people think of ad spend as simple and predictable, like a subscription payment. But platforms like Google Ads and Meta Ads sometimes flag accounts for manual review, freeze spend, or reverse charges after the fact if they suspect policy violations or fraud. If $10,000 of your planned $15,000 minimum spend gets reversed two days before your deadline, you might miss the bonus entirely.

This is a real risk for people running ad spend across many client accounts or newer ad accounts without a long track record. Newer accounts get flagged more often than accounts with years of clean history.

A common approach is to build in extra spend cushion above the stated minimum, especially near the deadline. If the card requires $15,000, some people aim to hit $17,000 or $18,000 with a week or more left before the deadline, in case something gets reversed or delayed in posting.

To avoid this mistake:

- Don't wait until the last few days of your minimum spend window to hit the target.
- Keep a buffer above the stated minimum if your ad accounts are newer or have had past holds.
- Check your card's posted transactions regularly during the bonus window, not just at the end.

## Am I Choosing Annual Fee Cards Without Checking the Real Return?

People sometimes grab a card with a high annual fee because the ad spend bonus category looks great, without doing the math on whether the extra points are worth the fee. This mistake shows up most with premium business cards.

This happens because a card advertising 4x points on advertising sounds obviously better than 1x. But if the annual fee is $295 and you're only running $20,000 a year in ad spend, the extra 3x points on that spend may not cover the fee, depending on how you value the points.

For example, 3x extra points on $20,000 is 60,000 extra points a year. If those points are worth around 1 cent each in a simple redemption, that's $600 in extra value, which does clear a $295 fee. But if you value the points lower, or you're not able to redeem them well, the math looks different. This is why point values are never guaranteed and depend heavily on how you actually redeem them.

A common approach is to run the math with your own real spend numbers and a conservative point value estimate before applying, not after.

To avoid this mistake:

- Calculate the annual fee against your realistic ad spend volume, not your best-case guess.
- Use a conservative point value when estimating returns, since actual redemption value varies by person and by redemption method.
- Compare no-annual-fee business cards as a baseline before assuming a premium card is worth it.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best credit card for ad spend?
The Chase Ink Business Preferred is generally considered the best card for ad spend, offering 3x points on the first $150,000 spent annually across combined categories including advertising purchases made with social media sites and search engines. It also earns a large sign-up bonus and points transfer to valuable airline and hotel partners. For business owners running large digital ad campaigns, this card maximizes both category bonuses and redemption flexibility, making it a top pick for marketers, agencies, and e-commerce sellers.

### Does ad spend count toward Chase 5/24?
Yes, opening a business credit card for ad spend still counts toward your Chase 5/24 status, since Chase counts nearly all personal and business cards (except a few from other banks) opened in the past 24 months. Even if you're applying specifically to earn rewards on advertising purchases, the account itself adds to your 5/24 count. This matters because exceeding five new accounts in 24 months can make you ineligible for future Chase card approvals, so plan ad-spend card applications carefully around your 5/24 timeline.

### How can I maximize sign-up bonuses using ad spend?
You can maximize sign-up bonuses by timing large, planned ad campaigns to coincide with a new card's minimum spending requirement window. Since ad spend is often predictable and substantial, it's an efficient way to hit bonus thresholds, like $6,000-$15,000 in 3 months, without altering your natural spending habits. This strategy works especially well for agencies and marketers with recurring monthly ad budgets, letting you unlock lucrative welcome bonuses on cards like the Ink Business Preferred or Amex Business Gold faster.

### Which card earns the most points on Facebook and Google ads?
The Amex Business Gold Card and Chase Ink Business Preferred both earn elevated rewards on advertising purchases, including Facebook and Google ads. Amex Business Gold offers 4x Membership Rewards points on the two categories where you spend the most each billing cycle, often including advertising, up to $150,000 combined per year. Chase Ink Business Preferred earns a flat 3x points on advertising purchases with no need to track spending categories, making it simpler for consistent, high-volume digital ad spenders.

### Should I get a personal or business card for ad spend?
A business card is typically better for ad spend because it separates business expenses, may offer higher credit limits, and often provides better rewards rates on advertising categories specifically. Business cards from Chase, Amex, and others also don't always appear on personal credit reports, which can help manage your Chase 5/24 count more strategically. However, personal cards can still be useful if you lack established business credit or prefer bonuses categories overlapping with personal spending needs.

# The Most Popular Credit Cards (And Why "Popular" Isn't Always the Right Pick for You)

The most popular cards right now are the Chase Sapphire Preferred, Chase Sapphire Reserve, Amex Gold, Amex Platinum, and Chase Freedom Unlimited. They're popular because they earn strong points, have wide appeal, and get talked about a lot online. But popular doesn't mean best for your 5/24 count, your spending, or your goals.

## Why Does "Most Popular" Cause Problems for Points People?

Popular cards get recommended everywhere, so people grab them without checking if they fit their own plan. That can waste a Chase 5/24 slot or lock up an annual fee you didn't need yet.

Here's the trap. You search "best credit card 2024" and five sites all list the same handful of cards. Chase Sapphire Preferred. Amex Gold. Capital One Venture X. They get repeated so often that they start to feel like the only options worth having.

But every recommendation list is written for a general audience. It doesn't know your Chase 5/24 count. It doesn't know you already have three travel cards and don't need a fourth. It doesn't know your goal is a trip to Japan in December, not generic "points."

For someone tracking 5/24, this matters more than for a casual cardholder. Chase counts personal card openings from all banks in the last 24 months. If you spend two of your five slots on popular Chase cards you don't fully use, you might miss out on other Chase cards you actually wanted more, like a Chase Ink Business card that doesn't count against personal 5/24 in the same way, or a card with a better bonus for your situation.

Popularity also creates a herd effect. When a card is everywhere on social media, the churn and burn crowd assumes it's the obvious next application. But a card that's great for a family who flies Southwest twice a year might be the wrong fit for a solo traveler who mainly wants cash back or hotel nights.

## Which Cards Are Actually the Most Popular Right Now?

The current heavy hitters are Chase Sapphire Preferred, Chase Sapphire Reserve, Amex Gold, Amex Platinum, Capital One Venture X, and Chase Freedom Unlimited. Each one is popular for a different reason, so it helps to know why before assuming one fits you.

**Chase Sapphire Preferred.** This is often called the best beginner points card. It has a moderate annual fee, earns 2x on travel and dining, and points transfer to airline and hotel partners like United, Southwest, Hyatt, and IHG. It's popular because it's the easiest entry point into transferable points.

**Chase Sapphire Reserve.** The step up from the Preferred. Higher annual fee, but it comes with a travel credit, airport lounge access through Priority Pass, and a higher earning rate on travel and dining. Popular with people who travel enough to use the credits and lounge access.

**Amex Gold.** Known for strong earning on groceries and restaurants. A lot of people who don't travel much still carry this card because of the everyday spending categories. It also has some credits that can offset the annual fee if you actually use them.

**Amex Platinum.** The card people talk about because of lounge access, hotel status, and a long list of credits. It has one of the higher annual fees in the mainstream card world, and its value depends heavily on whether you use the specific credits it offers.

**Capital One Venture X.** Newer to the "most popular" conversation but climbing fast. Lower annual fee than the Amex Platinum, still offers lounge access, and gives a flat travel credit that offsets much of the fee for many cardholders.

**Chase Freedom Unlimited.** The quiet workhorse. No annual fee, flat cash back rate, and it can combine with a Sapphire card to unlock better point value. Popular because it costs nothing to hold long term.

Notice a pattern. Four of these six are Chase cards. That's not an accident. Chase's transferable points system, paired with the 5/24 rule, makes timing your Chase applications one of the most important decisions in this hobby.

## Should You Just Get the Popular Cards Everyone Recommends?

Not automatically. A common approach is to check three things first: your 5/24 count, your actual spending habits, and whether you can realistically hit the sign-up bonus. Skipping this step is how people end up with cards that sit unused in a drawer.

Start with your 5/24 count. If you're at 3/24 or 4/24, a Chase card should usually take priority over comparable non-Chase cards, because your window to apply for Chase products is closing. If you're at 0/24 or 1/24, you have more room to be picky and can consider whether an Amex or Capital One card makes sense first, since those don't count against your Chase 5/24 total.

Next, look at your spending. The Amex Gold's strength is groceries and dining. If you spend very little on either, that card's everyday value drops, even though it's popular. The Chase Freedom Unlimited's strength is flat cash back with no annual fee. If you already have a strong cash back card, adding another one might not move the needle.

Then look at the sign-up bonus requirement. Popular cards often carry meaningful minimum spend requirements to earn the bonus, sometimes several thousand dollars within three months. If your monthly spending doesn't naturally reach that number, you'd need to plan ahead, whether that means timing a large planned purchase or being honest that this isn't the right month to apply.

A helpful gut check: would you still want this card if the sign-up bonus didn't exist? If the answer is no, the card might be better saved for later, or skipped in favor of something that fits your spending better.

## How Do You Pick Between Two Popular Cards That Look Similar?

Compare them on three things: the transfer partners you'd actually use, the credits you'd actually redeem, and how the annual fee compares to your realistic usage. Whichever card wins two out of three is usually the better pick for you.

Take Chase Sapphire Preferred versus Capital One Venture X. Both offer transferable points, but to different partner lists. Chase transfers to Hyatt, United, Southwest, and others. Capital One transfers to Turkish Airlines, Air France/KLM, Wyndham, and others. If you fly United regularly, Chase's ecosystem probably serves you better. If you've never used Turkish Airlines miles and don't plan to start, that transfer option means nothing to you personally.

Take Amex Gold versus Amex Platinum. The Gold has a lower fee and strong dining and grocery earning. The Platinum has a much longer list of credits, from airline incidental credits to hotel program credits to entertainment credits. On paper, the Platinum can look like more value. But those credits only count if you use them. Someone who doesn't fly often enough to use an airline incidental credit is leaving that part of the fee on the table.

The credits question deserves its own gut check. Make a simple list of every credit a card offers. Then be honest about how many you'd actually use in a normal year. If you'd use two out of six credits, the math on that card looks very different than the marketing math that assumes you use all six.

## Does It Matter What Order You Apply for Popular Cards?

Yes. Order matters a lot when Chase 5/24 is involved. A common approach is to apply for Chase cards first, while your 5/24 count is low, then apply for Amex or Capital One cards after, since those generally don't factor into Chase's rule the same way.

Here's the logic. Chase looks at how many personal cards you've opened, from any bank, in the trailing 24 months. Amex and Capital One don't have a rule like this for personal cards. So if you open three non-Chase cards first, you may have burned through slots that could have gone toward Chase products, without gaining anything back for Amex or Capital One eligibility.

A common sequence people describe looks something like this. Apply for the Chase card you want most first, since you're likely under 5/24. Wait for the required time between Chase applications, since Chase also has separate rules for how often you can open new cards or receive certain bonuses again. Then move to Amex or Capital One products once your Chase goals are set, or once you've crossed 5/24 and Chase products are largely out of reach anyway.

This doesn't mean non-Chase cards are worthless once you're over 5/24. It's the opposite. Being over 5/24 is actually a reasonable time to focus on Amex and Capital One, since Chase products are mostly off the table anyway, and there's no 5/24-style penalty for grabbing an Amex Gold or Venture X later.

## What's the Real Takeaway on Popular Cards?

Popular cards earned their reputation for real reasons. Strong points, useful transfer partners, and broad appeal. But "popular" is a description, not a recommendation. The right question isn't "which card does everyone have." It's "which card fits my 5/24 count, my spending, and my travel goals right now."

A simple way to sort this out: write down your current 5/24 count, your top two spending categories, and one trip or goal you're saving points for. Then check each popular card against that short list. The card that matches on all three is usually worth pursuing. The one that only matches on reputation is usually worth waiting on.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the Chase 5/24 rule?
The Chase 5/24 rule means Chase will typically deny your application for a new credit card if you've opened 5 or more personal credit cards (from any bank) within the past 24 months. This unofficial policy applies to most Chase credit cards, including popular travel rewards cards like the Sapphire Preferred and Ink Business cards. It's designed to limit approvals for people who churn cards frequently. Because of this rule, many credit card strategists recommend applying for Chase cards first, before pursuing cards from other issuers that report to your credit count.

### Which credit card has the best sign-up bonus right now?
The best sign-up bonus depends on your spending habits and travel goals, but cards like the Chase Sapphire Preferred, Capital One Venture X, and Amex Platinum consistently rank among the most popular for their high-value welcome offers. These bonuses often range from 60,000 to 100,000+ points or miles after meeting a minimum spend requirement within the first 3 months. Since offers change frequently, it's best to compare current bonus values, annual fees, and redemption options before applying to ensure the card fits your travel or cash-back goals.

### What order should I apply for credit cards to maximize bonuses?
Apply for Chase credit cards first, since Chase enforces the 5/24 rule and will reject applicants who already have too many recently opened accounts. Once you've secured the Chase cards you want, move on to issuers like American Express, Capital One, or Citi, which have more flexible approval policies. This strategy, often called "Chase first" in the points and miles community, helps you avoid missing out on valuable Chase sign-up bonuses like those from the Sapphire or Ink lineup simply because your 5/24 count is too high.

### How many credit cards can I have and stay under 5/24?
You can have more than 5 total credit cards and still qualify under 5/24, as long as no more than 5 new personal credit cards were opened in the trailing 24 months. Business credit cards from most issuers don't count toward your 5/24 status, though Chase business cards themselves are still restricted by the rule. Authorized user accounts can also count against you, so it's worth monitoring your credit report closely. Many cardholders track their 5/24 status using tools like 524tracker.com to plan future applications strategically.

### Are travel rewards credit cards worth the annual fee?
Travel rewards credit cards are often worth the annual fee if you regularly use their travel credits, lounge access, or bonus point categories to offset the cost. Cards like the Chase Sapphire Reserve or Amex Platinum charge $300–$700+ annually but can return that value through statement credits, elevated earning rates, and premium perks. If you rarely travel or won't use the included benefits, a no-annual-fee card or a lower-fee option may deliver better net value while still earning solid points or cash back on everyday purchases.

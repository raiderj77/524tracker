# Best Category Credit Cards: How to Stop Leaving Points on the Table

Most people use one card for everything. That means a flat 1-2% back, even on grocery runs and gas fill-ups that could earn 3x, 4x, or more. The fix is building a small stack of category cards, each one covering the spending where it earns the most, without going overboard on annual fees or new accounts.

## Why Am I Still Using One Card for Everything?

A lot of people stick with one card because switching feels like extra work. But that habit quietly costs real money every month. If you spend $500 on groceries with a flat 2% card, you earn $10. A grocery-focused card earning 4x could turn that into $20 worth of points or cash.

The gap adds up fast. Someone spending $1,500 a month across groceries, gas, and dining could be leaving $200 or more a year on the table just by using the wrong card in the wrong spot. That is real money, not a rounding error.

The other reason people stick to one card: they do not want to deal with Chase 5/24 or other bank rules that limit how often they can open new accounts. That is a fair concern. But you do not need ten cards to fix this. You usually need three, maybe four, chosen on purpose.

## What Even Counts as a "Category Card"?

A category card is any card that pays a higher rate on specific types of spending, like groceries, gas, dining, or travel. It is different from a flat-rate card, which pays the same percentage on everything.

Category cards usually work in one of two ways.

**Fixed bonus categories.** These cards always earn extra in the same spots. Groceries, gas, and dining are the most common. The Blue Cash Preferred from American Express is a well-known example, with a strong rate on U.S. supermarkets up to a spending cap.

**Rotating bonus categories.** These cards change their bonus category every three months. Discover it and the Chase Freedom Flex both use this model. One quarter might be gas stations and gyms. The next might be Amazon and grocery stores. You have to activate the category each quarter, and there is usually a spending cap on the bonus rate.

Both types can work well. Fixed cards are simpler. Rotating cards can pay more, but they take more attention.

## How Do I Pick Categories That Actually Match My Spending?

Look at three months of real statements before picking any card. Add up what you spent in groceries, gas, dining, travel, and everything else. Whatever categories show up biggest, that is where you shop for a card.

This sounds obvious, but most people skip it. They pick a card because a blog post said it was "the best," without checking if that card's bonus category matches their own life. A card that pays 5x on office supply stores does nothing for someone who never buys office supplies.

A simple way to do this: pull up the last three statements from your main card. Sort spending into buckets like groceries, gas, dining, travel, and general purchases. Whatever bucket is the biggest gets the first category card. The second-biggest bucket usually determines the second card.

For example, someone who spends heavily on groceries and a moderate amount on gas might build a stack like this:

- A grocery card for supermarket spending
- A gas card for fuel purchases
- A flat 2% card for everything that does not fit a category

That third card matters. It is the safety net for all the odd purchases that do not fit neatly anywhere else.

## Do I Need a Different Card for Every Category?

No. Most people find two to four cards covers almost everything. Trying to hold a separate card for every possible category creates more hassle than it is worth.

The goal is coverage, not collection. A common approach looks like this:

1. One card for groceries
2. One card for gas or transit
3. One card for dining or travel
4. One flat-rate card as a catch-all

That is four cards. Some people trim it to three by finding a card that covers two categories at once, like dining and travel together. Others expand to five if they have unusual spending, like frequent international travel or big transit costs.

The number matters less than the coverage. If a card in your wallet never gets used because its category rarely comes up in your life, it is not helping.

## How Do Bonus Categories Actually Get Capped?

Almost every bonus category has a spending limit before the rate drops. Missing this detail is one of the most common mistakes people make with category cards.

For example, a card might offer 4x on groceries, but only on the first $6,000 spent per year. After that, the rate might drop to 1x. If a household spends $9,000 a year on groceries, the last $3,000 earns the base rate, not the bonus rate.

Rotating category cards work similarly but reset every quarter. A card might offer 5x on a category, capped at $1,500 in combined purchases each quarter. Spend more than that in the category, and the extra spending earns only 1x.

This is why checking the cap matters as much as checking the rate. A high reward rate with a low cap can end up paying less over a year than a lower rate with no cap at all.

## Should I Worry About Annual Fees on Category Cards?

Annual fees can make sense, but only if the extra rewards clearly cover the fee. A simple gut check: add up the extra rewards a card would likely earn in a year, based on real spending, then compare that number to the fee.

Say a grocery card charges a $95 annual fee but offers a much higher rate than a no-fee alternative. If someone spends $6,000 a year on groceries, the difference between a 3x card and a 6x card could be worth well over $95 in extra rewards, depending on how those points get valued. In that case, the fee often pays for itself.

But if grocery spending is only $2,000 a year, the math might not work out the same way. The fee could eat most or all of the extra rewards.

A common approach is to start with no-annual-fee category cards first. Once spending patterns are clear and stable, some people upgrade to fee-based cards where the math clearly favors it.

## How Does This Fit with Chase 5/24?

Every new card application counts against the 5/24 count, category cards included. That means people chasing Chase cards and sign-up bonuses need to think carefully about order.

A common strategy is to apply for Chase cards first, since Chase is the strictest about counting recent accounts. Category cards from issuers that do not follow 5/24 rules, like American Express or Citi, can often be added later without the same restriction.

For example, someone planning to eventually get a Chase Sapphire Preferred or a Chase Ink card might hold off on opening several small store cards or gas cards from other banks first. Adding those non-Chase category cards after locking in the 5/24-sensitive cards is often the smoother path.

This is not universal advice, since goals differ. Someone who does not care about Chase cards at all does not need to think about 5/24 order the same way.

## Do I Need to Track All This Manually?

Not really. Most category card users rely on a simple system: a note on their phone, a spreadsheet, or a card-specific app reminder for quarterly activations.

The biggest failure point for rotating category cards is forgetting to activate the bonus category each quarter. Many issuers do not apply the bonus automatically. If the category is not activated, spending in that category might earn only the base rate.

A simple habit fixes this: set a recurring reminder on the first day of every quarter to check and activate categories on any rotating cards. It takes less than five minutes and prevents a real loss in earnings.

For fixed-category cards, there is far less to track. The rate applies automatically, so the main thing to watch is the annual spending cap.

## What's a Reasonable Way to Build This Stack Over Time?

Start small. Add one category card that matches your single biggest spending category. Use it for a few months. Then evaluate whether a second card makes sense.

Trying to build a full four-card stack all at once often backfires. It can trigger multiple hard inquiries at once, make 5/24 planning harder, and create confusion about which card to use where.

A more measured pace looks like this:

- Month 1: Add a card for your top spending category.
- Month 3-4: Review whether the card is earning as expected.
- Month 6: Add a second card for your next-largest category, if it still makes sense.

This slower pace also gives time to see whether spending habits actually match assumptions. Sometimes a category that looked big on paper turns out to be smaller in daily life, or vice versa.

## What's the Simplest Way to Think About All This?

Match the card to the spending, not the other way around. A category card only helps if it lines up with money you were already going to spend. Chasing a card because of hype, without checking real spending habits, is how wallets end up full of cards that barely get used.

The strongest category card stacks are boring, in a good way. A grocery card that gets used every week. A gas card that gets used on every fill-up. A flat-rate card that quietly catches everything else. Nothing fancy, just steady extra rewards on spending that was already happening.

That is the whole point of category cards. Not to change how much you spend, but to make sure the spending you were already doing works a little harder for you.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best credit card for dining and travel purchases?
The Chase Sapphire Reserve or American Express Gold Card are top picks for dining and travel due to their high multipliers of 3-4x points per dollar. These cards consistently outperform flat-rate cards in bonus categories, letting you accumulate points faster for redemptions. However, before applying, check your Chase 5/24 status, since Chase-issued cards will be denied if you've opened five or more cards across all banks in the past 24 months, regardless of your credit score or income.

### Does applying for a category credit card count toward Chase 5/24?
Yes, any personal credit card opened counts toward your 5/24 count, whether it's a Chase card or from another issuer like Amex or Capital One. Business cards from most banks (except Chase's own business cards in some cases) typically don't report to personal credit reports and often don't count. Before applying for a new category card, use a tracker to count your approvals in the trailing 24 months so you don't accidentally trigger a denial on a valuable Chase application later.

### Should I get a category card or a flat-rate rewards card first?
Prioritize category cards first if you're under 5/24 and eligible for Chase products, since bonus categories like dining, groceries, and travel offer higher long-term value than flat 1.5-2x cards. Flat-rate cards are best reserved for spending that doesn't fit any bonus category. Sequencing matters because once you hit 5/24, you lose access to popular Chase category cards like the Sapphire Preferred, so apply for those first while you're still eligible.

### How do I maximize sign-up bonuses without hurting my 5/24 status?
Focus on Chase cards first while you're under 5/24, then move to non-Chase issuers like Amex, Citi, or Capital One afterward since they don't restrict approvals based on 5/24. This sequencing preserves your access to Chase's valuable category cards and bonuses. Space out applications by a few months to protect your credit score and avoid issuer-specific rules like Amex's once-per-lifetime bonus policy or Chase's 48-month rule on the Sapphire cards.

### Which category card offers the best value for groceries?
The American Express Blue Cash Preferred and Amex Gold Card both offer strong grocery category earnings, with rates up to 4-6x points or cash back at U.S. supermarkets. These rates far exceed typical 1-2x flat cards, making them ideal if groceries are a major spending category for your household. Since Amex doesn't factor into Chase's 5/24 rule, you can pursue these grocery-focused cards independently without affecting your eligibility for future Chase applications.
